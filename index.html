Unnumbered points within a chapter are identified with letters, such as 2.5.A or 4.3.B, making each topic easier to locate and reference. Where a topic appears in more than one chapter, its full explanation is provided once and the related section approaches it from a different perspective.

The strategic origin: the term "Industrie 4.0" was launched in 2011 as part of the German government's High-Tech Strategy, and a working group led by acatech (the National Academy of Science and Engineering) turned it into a blueprint in 2013, with the aim of keeping Germany at the top of global manufacturing.

1.1 Understanding the Fourth Industrial Revolution

This section places Industrie 4.0 in its historical, technological and international context: where the idea came from, what the four revolutions were, which technologies define the fourth, why Germany led, and how it compares with the USA, China and Japan.

1.1.1 What is Industrie 4.0? Definition, origin and historical context

Industrie 4.0 is the German name for the digital transformation of manufacturing: the idea that factories, machines, products, people and suppliers are connected through the internet and software, so that production can organise and optimise itself. The term was introduced at the Hannover Messe trade fair in 2011 as part of the federal government's High-Tech Strategy 2020. A working group of industry and academia, led by Henning Kagermann (acatech) and Siegfried Dais (Bosch), turned it into a concrete blueprint, and its recommendations were handed to the government in April 2013. The number '4.0' is borrowed from software versions to signal the fourth major step in industrial history.

The motivation was strategic. Germany is a high-wage, export-oriented country with a world-leading machinery industry. Policy-makers feared that US internet companies and low-cost Asian producers could erode that position unless German industry combined its engineering strength with modern information technology. The wider phrase 'Fourth Industrial Revolution' was later popularised internationally by Klaus Schwab of the World Economic Forum (2016) and covers more than manufacturing.

1.1.2 Steam engine (Industry 1.0)

The first industrial revolution (c. 1760–1840) replaced human and animal muscle with water and steam power. James Watt's improvements to the steam engine (from 1769) made power available anywhere, not only beside a river, so production moved into factories and growing cities. Mechanised spinning and weaving machines multiplied output, coal and iron became strategic resources, and railways linked markets.

Germany industrialised later than Britain. The Zollverein customs union (1834), the first German railway between Nuremberg and Fürth (1835) and companies such as Borsig and Krupp drove its catch-up. The social effects were profound: urbanisation, wage labour, child labour and, in response, the first labour laws and trade unions.

Example: A steam-driven power loom could do the work of many hand weavers, which is why textile output in Britain, and later in Saxony and the Rhineland, rose so quickly.

1.1.3 Electricity and mass production (Industry 2.0)

From about 1870, electricity, the internal-combustion engine and new chemistry (steel, dyes, fertiliser) powered the second industrial revolution. Electric motors gave each machine its own drive instead of a central steam shaft, so factory layouts could be redesigned. Management ideas such as Frederick Taylor's scientific management and Henry Ford's moving assembly line (1913) introduced strict division of labour and standardised parts, making goods such as cars cheap enough for ordinary families.

German firms led in electrical engineering and chemicals: Siemens and AEG in electricity, and BASF with the Haber–Bosch process for synthetic ammonia, which went into industrial production in 1913.

Example: Ford cut the assembly time of a Model T chassis from more than 12 hours to about 1.5 hours after introducing the moving line, and the price of the car fell as volumes rose.

1.1.4 Computers and automation (Industry 3.0)

The third industrial revolution (from the late 1960s) used electronics and information technology to automate production. The programmable logic controller (PLC, first introduced in 1969) replaced relay cabinets, CNC machines followed numerical programs, and industrial robots such as Unimate (1961) and KUKA's FAMULUS (1973, the first six-axis electromechanical robot) took over repetitive tasks. Software for planning (MRP/ERP) and design (CAD/CAM) appeared.

The limits were structural. Automation consisted of isolated islands controlled from the top of a rigid hierarchy (the 'automation pyramid'), programmed for fixed tasks and poorly connected to suppliers, customers or each other. That rigidity is exactly what Industrie 4.0 tries to overcome.

Example: A car-body welding line of the 1990s could weld thousands of identical bodies with great precision, but changing to a new model meant weeks of reprogramming and rebuilding.

1.1.5 Cyber-physical systems (Industry 4.0)

Industry 4.0 connects the islands. Cheap sensors, ubiquitous networking, cloud computing and artificial intelligence allow machines, products and IT systems to exchange data continuously and to make local decisions. Instead of a rigid pyramid there is a network of cyber-physical systems, in which each element can describe itself digitally, find partners and adapt.

Three integration directions define it: vertical (shop floor to enterprise software), horizontal (across suppliers and customers) and end-to-end digital engineering across the product life cycle. The goal is flexible, efficient, resource-saving production of individualised products ('batch size one') at close to mass-production cost.

Example: In Siemens' Amberg plant each printed circuit board carries a code that tells the machines what to do with it, so about a thousand product variants can run on the same lines.

1.1.6 Definition and core principles of Industrie 4.0

Researchers Hermann, Pentek and Otto (2016), analysing the literature, summarised Industrie 4.0 as four design principles. Together they describe what makes a factory 'smart'.

1.1.7 The Internet of Things (IoT) and Industrial IoT (IIoT) in manufacturing

The Internet of Things (IoT) links physical objects that have sensors, small computers and network connections. In industry this becomes the Industrial IoT (IIoT): machines, drives, valves and tools stream status and process data to gateways, edge devices and cloud platforms in real time. IIoT differs from consumer IoT in its demands on reliability, determinism, safety and security: a failed smart bulb is an annoyance, a failed press controller is a hazard.

A typical architecture runs from sensor or actuator, via a gateway or edge device, to a platform (storage, analytics) and then to applications (dashboards, maintenance, planning). Common communication standards are OPC UA, MQTT and fieldbuses such as PROFINET.

Example: Kaeser Kompressoren's compressors report pressure, temperature and energy use to a central system. This allows the company to sell compressed air by the cubic metre and to schedule maintenance before a breakdown.

1.1.8 Big data and cloud manufacturing

Connected factories generate large, fast and varied data: the classic '3 Vs' of volume, velocity and variety, plus veracity. Big data technologies (time-series databases, data lakes, stream processing) collect, store and analyse them to find patterns that people cannot see. Cloud manufacturing delivers computing power and software as a service over the network, and extends to sharing production capacity: design, simulation, planning and even machine time can be ordered on demand.

Benefits are scalability and lower IT cost, especially for SMEs. Concerns are latency, data sovereignty and vendor lock-in, which is why German initiatives such as Gaia-X and Catena-X promote trusted, federated data spaces.

Example: Siemens' cloud platform Insights Hub (formerly MindSphere) lets a machine builder collect data from machines installed at customers' sites around the world and compare the performance of the whole fleet.

1.1.9 Additive manufacturing (3D printing)

Additive manufacturing (AM, or '3D printing') builds parts layer by layer from a digital model, in polymers, metals or ceramics, instead of cutting material away. Processes include fused deposition modelling, stereolithography, selective laser sintering and melting, and binder jetting.

For Industrie 4.0 its strengths are on-demand production, rapid prototyping and customisation: no tooling is needed, so a batch of one costs almost as much per part as a batch of a thousand; complex lightweight shapes become possible; and digital spare-parts warehouses can replace physical stock. Germany is also a leading supplier of the machines (EOS, Trumpf, Nikon SLM Solutions, voxeljet, BigRep).

Example: Adidas' Futurecraft 4D midsole (2017) was made by light-based 3D printing together with the company Carbon, and Siemens Energy uses additive manufacturing to produce and repair gas-turbine components.

1.1.10 Cyber-physical systems (CPS)

A cyber-physical system tightly integrates computation, networking and physical processes: embedded sensors observe the real world, software analyses the data and decides, and actuators act back on the process in a closed loop. Examples range from a smart machine tool to a whole production cell, a smart power grid or an autonomous vehicle.

Lee, Bagheri and Kao's '5C architecture' (Connection, Conversion, Cyber, Cognition, Configuration) describes maturity levels from simple data acquisition up to self-configuring systems. CPS are the building blocks of Industrie 4.0 because each one can carry its own digital description, communicate with others and take local decisions.

Example: A CNC machine that measures tool wear while cutting, compares it with a digital model and automatically adjusts its feed rate, or orders a new tool, is a cyber-physical system.

1.1.11 Why Germany? Mittelstand, engineering excellence and exports

Several features made Germany the birthplace of Industrie 4.0. First, the Mittelstand: about 99% of German companies are small or medium-sized, and many are family-owned 'hidden champions' that dominate world niches in machinery and components. Second, engineering excellence: manufacturing contributes roughly a fifth of gross value added, much more than in the USA or UK, and Germany is a leading supplier of machines and automation.

Third, an export-driven economy: exports amount to close to half of economic output, so staying globally competitive is existential. Fourth, a research and training system (Fraunhofer institutes, technical universities, dual apprenticeships) and pressures such as high wages and an ageing workforce that make productivity gains essential.

Example: A family firm in Swabia that makes one kind of industrial filter may supply a large share of world demand. It has the engineering depth but few IT specialists, which is exactly the gap that Industrie 4.0 support programmes try to close.

1.1.12 Government initiatives: High-Tech Strategy and Platform Industrie 4.0

Industrie 4.0 is unusually well supported by policy. It began as a 'future project' of the High-Tech Strategy 2020 (2010). The Platform Industrie 4.0 was founded in 2013 by the industry associations Bitkom, VDMA and ZVEI and relaunched in 2015 together with the ministries, trade unions and research institutes. The High-Tech Strategy 2025 (adopted 2018) framed research funding around missions such as digitalisation and AI, and in 2023 it was followed by the Future Research and Innovation Strategy.

The federal economics and research ministries fund research programmes, testbeds and SME support such as Mittelstand-Digital, while the Digital Strategy and AI Strategy provide the wider framework.

Example: The Manufacturing-X programme (2023), which funds data spaces for industry with up to about €150 million, grew out of Platform Industrie 4.0 and builds on the Catena-X automotive network.

1.1.13 Key players: Siemens, Bosch, SAP, Festo, Trumpf and KUKA

A handful of German companies act as technology providers, platform builders and early adopters. Siemens supplies automation hardware and industrial software and runs the Amberg showcase plant. Bosch has its Connected Industry business and Nexeed software, with lead plants such as Homburg and Blaichach. SAP provides the enterprise software backbone (ERP and manufacturing execution). Festo offers pneumatic and electric automation, training and the Bionic Learning Network. Trumpf builds laser machine tools and connected-factory software. KUKA makes industrial robots and has belonged to China's Midea since 2017.

Around them stand SMEs such as Beckhoff and Phoenix Contact, research bodies such as Fraunhofer and the DFKI, and the associations VDMA, ZVEI and Bitkom.

Example: A single smart factory can easily use all four layers: Siemens controllers and software, SAP for orders and finance, KUKA robots and Festo actuators.

1.1.14 Global comparison: Germany vs. USA (Industrial Internet Consortium)

The US approach grew out of the Industrial Internet Consortium (IIC), founded in 2014 by AT&T, Cisco, GE, IBM and Intel (since 2021 it operates as the Industry IoT Consortium within the Object Management Group). It is software- and platform-led: cloud analytics, data-driven services and start-up energy, building on Silicon Valley's strengths. Germany's approach is engineering- and production-led: standardised architectures (RAMI 4.0), equipment excellence and the inclusion of SMEs.

The two are complementary. The reference architectures RAMI 4.0 and the IIC's IIRA have been compared and mapped to each other, and both sides cooperate on testbeds and standards. Germany's weak spot is the lack of consumer-scale platforms and hyperscale cloud providers of its own.

Example: GE's Predix platform (software first) contrasts with Siemens' automation-first approach. GE later scaled Predix back, which shows that platform ambitions are hard to realise even for giants.

1.1.15 Germany vs. China (Made in China 2025)

Made in China 2025, launched in 2015, is a state-led ten-year plan to move Chinese manufacturing up the value chain: robotics, new-energy vehicles, aerospace, advanced machine tools and other priority sectors, with higher domestic content in core components. It was inspired by Industrie 4.0 but relies on state direction, subsidies and scale, whereas Germany relies on companies, associations and open standards.

China is both Germany's biggest trading partner and a rising competitor. The 2016–17 takeover of the robot maker KUKA by Midea fuelled debate about technology transfer, and in International Federation of Robotics statistics for 2023 China's robot density (robots per 10,000 employees) overtook Germany's.

Example: German carmakers increasingly develop 'in China, for China', while Chinese electric-vehicle makers such as BYD compete in Europe. This two-way pressure shapes Germany's 'de-risking' debate.

1.1.16 Germany vs. Japan (Society 5.0)

Japan's Society 5.0, introduced in the 5th Science and Technology Basic Plan (2016), describes a 'super-smart society' that follows the hunter-gatherer, agrarian, industrial and information societies. It uses IoT, AI and robotics to solve social problems such as an ageing population, rural decline and disasters, so its scope is broader and more human-centred than the production focus of Industrie 4.0.

Japanese firms, organised for example in the Industrial Value Chain Initiative (IVI), favour a loosely coupled, bottom-up approach with a strong shop-floor (Kaizen) culture and a leading robotics industry. The two countries cooperate: they signed the Hannover Declaration on IoT and Industrie 4.0 cooperation in 2017.

Example: Fanuc and Yaskawa robots from Japan are used in many German plants, while German controllers and software run in Japanese ones. Society 5.0 projects also cover elderly care, which Industrie 4.0 does not.

1.2 Smart Technologies Driving the Revolution

Five technology families make the smart factory possible: connected devices, cloud and edge computing, data analytics, real-time processing and fast wireless communication.

1.2.1 Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)

A typical IIoT deployment has four layers: devices (sensors, drives, controllers), connectivity (industrial Ethernet, wireless, 5G), a platform (device management, data storage, analytics, security) and applications (monitoring, maintenance, energy management). The biggest practical challenge in Germany's SME-dominated industry is the brownfield: most machines in use are 15 to 30 years old and have no network interface, so they are retrofitted with add-on sensors and gateways.

IIoT value comes less from connecting things than from using the data to change decisions, such as when to service a machine or how to schedule a job.

Example: A turning shop fits clamp-on current sensors to ten old lathes and sends the data through a gateway to a cloud dashboard. Within weeks it can see which machines stand idle most often and why.

1.2.2 Cloud computing and edge computing

Cloud computing offers elastic storage and computing power over the internet, which is ideal for large-scale analytics, model training and fleet-wide comparison. Edge computing places processing close to the machine, in gateways or industrial PCs, because some tasks need millisecond responses, produce too much data to ship continuously, or involve sensitive data that should not leave the plant. Most real systems are hybrid: the edge filters data and runs trained models, and the cloud trains models and coordinates plants. 'Fog computing' describes layers between the two.

1.2.3 Big data and analytics

Industrial data come from machines (sensor streams, alarms), enterprise systems (orders, bills of materials), quality tests and the environment. Analytics turn them into insight at four levels: descriptive (what happened), diagnostic (why), predictive (what will happen) and prescriptive (what to do); see 3.5.1. The challenges are heterogeneous formats, poor data quality, missing labels, and the need to combine domain knowledge with data science.

Siemens reports that the Amberg plant generates about 50 million process and product data items a day, all feeding quality and planning decisions. Without analytics such data are only a storage cost.

Example: By correlating oven temperature curves with later failure rates, a manufacturer finds that a drift of 3 °C in one zone doubles the scrap rate, and adds an automatic alarm for it.

1.2.4 Real-time data processing

'Real-time' means that the answer arrives within a deadline that matters to the process. Hard real-time (microseconds to milliseconds) is needed for motion control and safety functions; near-real-time (seconds) is enough for dashboards and alerts. Technologies include deterministic industrial Ethernet (PROFINET IRT, EtherCAT), Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN), stream-processing frameworks such as Apache Kafka and Flink, and in-memory databases.

Real-time data allow closed-loop control, instant alarms and dynamic scheduling instead of after-the-fact reports.

Example: If a press-force curve deviates from its reference during a stroke, a real-time system stops the next stroke at once and protects the die. A nightly report would only reveal the problem after hundreds of faulty parts.

1.2.5 Wireless communication (5G in industry)

Industrial 5G offers high bandwidth (eMBB), ultra-reliable low latency (URLLC, in the range of 1 to 10 ms) and massive device density (mMTC). Private campus networks give a factory its own 5G network, separate from public operators. Germany reserved the 3.7–3.8 GHz band for local industrial networks (applications from 2019), a distinctive policy choice that lets even mid-sized companies run their own 5G.

Uses include untethered AGVs and robots, AR headsets, wireless sensors and flexible reconfiguration without new cabling. The caveats are device availability, coverage planning and cost; wired networks remain the choice for safety-critical control.

Example: Mercedes-Benz's Factory 56 in Sindelfingen was built with an on-site 5G network connected to assembly and logistics, and Bosch operates 5G test networks in its own plants.

1.3 Smart Factory Concepts

A smart factory applies the technologies above to run itself more effectively: it adjusts its own processes, anticipates failures, monitors everything, decides on its own within limits and mirrors itself in a digital twin.

1.3.1 Self-optimizing production systems

A self-optimising production system measures its own results, compares them with its goals (quality, speed, energy use) and adapts its parameters automatically: a closed feedback loop plus learning. Levels range from simple rule-based control, via statistical process control, to machine-learning models that find better setpoints than human operators. Prerequisites are reliable sensors, a digital model of the process, safe limits for automatic changes and operator trust.

Example: In injection moulding, the machine measures melt pressure in each cycle and adjusts the holding pressure to keep part weight constant despite changing material batches, which reduces scrap without any operator input.

1.3.2 Predictive maintenance

Maintenance strategies develop from reactive (repair after failure) to preventive (fixed intervals), predictive (act when data show deterioration) and prescriptive (the system also recommends the action). Predictive maintenance uses condition data such as vibration, temperature, motor current and acoustic emission, together with models that estimate the remaining useful life of a component.

Often-cited estimates, for example from McKinsey, say that it can cut unplanned downtime by 30 to 50 per cent and extend machine life, but the results depend heavily on data quality and on having enough examples of failures.

Example: The vibration spectrum of a bearing shows an early-defect frequency three weeks before failure, so the plant replaces it during a planned stop. In aviation, Lufthansa Technik's AVIATAR platform applies the same idea to aircraft fleets.

1.3.3 Smart monitoring and control

Monitoring makes the process visible; control acts on what is seen. Modern systems combine SCADA and HMI screens, MES data and dashboards, alerts on mobile devices and increasingly assistive analytics that rank alarms by importance to avoid 'alarm flooding'. Smart control goes further with model-predictive control, adaptive parameters and remote operation from central control rooms. The aim is management by exception: people intervene only when the system cannot cope.

Example: In the control room of a chemical site, instead of 500 simultaneous alarms the system shows the three most critical issues with suggested actions.

1.3.4 Autonomous decision-making systems

Autonomous systems decide and act without human input within defined limits. The spectrum runs from rule-based logic, via optimisation and machine learning, to multi-agent systems in which machines, workpieces and vehicles negotiate tasks among themselves. As with self-driving cars, 'levels of autonomy' describe how much control is delegated.

Safety, explainability and accountability are crucial: humans define the goals and boundaries, and critical decisions keep human oversight. The EU AI Act (2024) requires this for high-risk uses.

Example: At the SmartFactoryKL in Kaiserslautern, the 'Production Level 4' demonstration showed independent modules from many suppliers docking into one line, with each product choosing its route through the modules according to its customer order.

1.3.5 Digital twins and simulation

A digital twin is a virtual model of a physical asset, process or system that is kept in step with reality by data. A model without live data is a digital model; one that receives data automatically is a digital shadow; one that also influences the real object is a full digital twin. Twins support design (test before building), commissioning (virtual commissioning), operation (what-if analysis, predictive maintenance) and service. Simulation types include discrete-event (material flow), physics-based (FEM, CFD) and agent-based simulation.

Example: BMW developed virtual factories on NVIDIA's Omniverse platform, in which planners around the world can walk through and optimise a plant before it is built. Siemens uses twins of both the product and the production in Amberg.

1.4 Smart Supply Chain and Logistics

Industrie 4.0 does not stop at the factory gate. Smart logistics connects suppliers, plants, warehouses and customers so that goods and information flow together.

1.4.1 Intelligent logistics networks

Logistics networks become 'intelligent' when data flow between shippers, carriers, warehouses and customers, and algorithms use them to plan and re-plan routes and transport modes. Typical elements are a control tower with an end-to-end overview, predictive arrival times, dynamic routing, multimodal planning and automatic exception handling.

Germany is Europe's logistics hub, with the port of Hamburg, Frankfurt airport and a dense road and rail network, and German operators such as DHL, DB Schenker and Hapag-Lloyd invest heavily in digital platforms.

Example: The Port of Hamburg's smartPORT programme connects traffic lights, bridges, terminals and trucks, so that trucks are told in advance which terminal slot is free. That reduces queues and emissions.

1.4.2 Automated inventory management

Automatic inventory management reduces stock while avoiding shortages. Sensors, RFID and cameras count stock; an e-Kanban signal triggers replenishment when a bin runs low; algorithms set safety stock from demand forecasts and supplier reliability; and in vendor-managed inventory the supplier sees consumption and refills automatically. Benefits are lower working capital, fewer line stoppages and less manual counting.

Example: Würth's 'iBin' smart bin for small parts monitors its own fill level and triggers a re-order from the supplier, so workers no longer count screws.

1.4.3 Smart warehousing solutions

Smart warehouses combine automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS), shuttle systems, goods-to-person picking stations, AGVs or AMRs and robotic pickers, controlled by warehouse management software (WMS) with real-time stock data. Layout and slotting are optimised by algorithms, and digital twins test changes before they are made. Benefits are higher storage density, speed and accuracy and better ergonomics; constraints are the high investment and reduced flexibility if volumes change.

Example: In an automated e-commerce warehouse, mobile robots bring shelf units to a picker (goods-to-person). Amazon pioneered the approach, and German suppliers such as SSI Schäfer build comparable systems.

1.4.4 Real-time supply chain visibility

Visibility means knowing where goods are, in what condition and when they will arrive, across all tiers of suppliers. It is achieved with GPS and IoT trackers, scanning, electronic data interchange, API integrations and supply-chain platforms. It enables early warning of delays and disruptions and, increasingly, legal compliance: Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (in force since 2023) and EU rules demand knowledge of suppliers' human-rights and environmental practices. Data sharing between companies is the main hurdle, hence data spaces such as Catena-X.

Example: Through Catena-X, automotive suppliers can pass on the carbon footprint of a component along the chain, so that the carmaker can calculate a vehicle's footprint without every supplier disclosing confidential data.

1.4.5 Driverless transport systems

Driverless transport systems (German: Fahrerlose Transportsysteme, FTS) move material without drivers. AGVs follow fixed guidance such as magnetic tape, wires or reflectors; AMRs navigate freely using LiDAR and maps and avoid obstacles. Fleet-management software assigns missions and enforces traffic rules. The German VDA 5050 interface standard, issued by VDA and VDMA, lets vehicles from different manufacturers work under one control system. Applications include line-side supply, pallet transport and tugger trains.

Example: BMW and Mercedes-Benz use driverless vehicles to deliver parts to assembly stations just in time in plants such as Leipzig and Sindelfingen, replacing forklift trips and fixed conveyors.

1.5 Case Studies: Smart Revolution in Action

Three large German companies show different faces of Industrie 4.0: a technology supplier that is also its own test bed (Bosch), a process-industry giant (BASF) and a global carmaker building a group-wide platform (Volkswagen).

1.5.1 Bosch Connected Manufacturing

Bosch began its Industrie 4.0 journey in 2012 with a dual strategy: to be a lead user by connecting its own 250-plus plants and a lead provider by selling the solutions (Connected Industry, Nexeed software). Lead plants such as Homburg (hydraulics) and Blaichach (ABS/ESP brake control systems) tested the concepts.

At Blaichach the transformation, which started in 2013, networked thousands of machines (about 6,600 connected devices by 2021) and trained some 3,000 employees. According to Bosch, output of ABS and ESP systems tripled within six years without enlarging the plant. The lessons are to connect people as well as machines, to standardise data, and to scale from lead plants.

Example: At Blaichach, machines publish their status on a common network, so that variations in an upstream process are passed on automatically to downstream machines, which adjust, while employees see role-based KPI dashboards.

1.5.2 BASF Smart Production

BASF's Ludwigshafen site is the world's largest integrated chemical complex (the 'Verbund'), where plants exchange energy and materials. Chemical production is a process industry with continuous flows rather than discrete parts, so BASF's digitalisation focuses on sensors on pipes, pumps and tanks, predictive maintenance, plant simulation and AI-assisted optimisation.

In 2017 the company commissioned its own supercomputer, Quriosity, to simulate chemical processes and speed up the development of materials. Other uses are drones and augmented reality for inspections, digital twins of plants and data platforms that link research and production.

Example: Machine-learning models on pump and compressor data warn of impending failures, so that BASF can plan repairs during scheduled stops instead of risking an unplanned shutdown of an interlinked Verbund chain.

1.5.3 Volkswagen Digital Production

Volkswagen Group's Digital Production Platform, developed with Amazon Web Services and Siemens and announced in 2019, is intended to link all of its more than 120 plants and thousands of suppliers in an industrial cloud. The aim is to standardise data from machines and logistics and to run analytics and apps (for example for predictive maintenance, logistics and quality) once and roll them out worldwide, instead of each plant building its own solution.

Volkswagen also converted its Zwickau plant to all-electric MEB production (2019–20) and uses 3D printing and driverless vehicles. The challenges are legacy IT, the complexity of a global group and cost pressure, which is also why the company has restructured its German sites (see 5.5.B).

Example: A quality-analytics app developed for one plant can be deployed to other plants through the platform, whereas before each plant had its own incompatible data.

2.1 Vision and Strategic Goals

Germany's long-term aim is to become both the leading market and the leading supplier ('lead market, lead supplier') of Industrie 4.0 technology. This section covers the political frame, the Platform Industrie 4.0 and the economic goals.

2.1.1 German Government's Digital Agenda

The federal government's first Digital Agenda 2014–2017 set goals for broadband, IT security, digital skills and the digital economy, and named Industrie 4.0 as a key project. It was followed by a Digital Strategy (2022) and, since 2025, by a dedicated Federal Ministry for Digital and State Modernisation. Across these documents the recurring aims are fast fibre and mobile networks, a trustworthy legal framework (data protection, IT security), digital public services, support for start-ups and SMEs, and research in AI, quantum technology and microelectronics.

For industry the agenda matters because infrastructure, regulation and skills decide whether firms can actually adopt Industrie 4.0.

Example: The Gigabit Strategy (2022) aims at fibre connections for households and businesses and good 5G coverage by 2030, with industrial parks as priority areas.

2.1.2 Plattform Industrie 4.0 initiative

The Platform was founded in 2013 by the associations Bitkom, VDMA and ZVEI and relaunched in 2015 as a government-backed network of companies, research institutes, trade unions and ministries. It is Germany's central forum for coordinating Industrie 4.0: it writes recommendations, develops reference models and standards, supports SMEs and represents Germany internationally.

Its best-known results are the reference architecture RAMI 4.0, the Asset Administration Shell, the 'Industrie 4.0 Landscape' of projects and, more recently, the data-space initiative Manufacturing-X.

Example: The Platform's paper on the Asset Administration Shell led to an international standard (IEC 63278), which means a pump made in Germany and a controller made in Japan can describe themselves in the same way.

2.1.3 National competitiveness objectives

The 2013 recommendations proposed a dual strategy: Germany should become a lead market (using Industrie 4.0 widely at home) and a lead supplier (exporting the technology). Objectives include higher productivity and resource efficiency, flexible response to customers, keeping manufacturing jobs by moving to higher-value work, sustaining exports and giving SMEs access to the technology.

The objectives respond to competition from US platforms and Chinese scale, and to Germany's interest in keeping a broad industrial base rather than only a services economy.

Example: A German machine builder is a lead supplier when its machines come with standard digital interfaces and paid services. The same company acts as lead market when its own plants run on such systems.

2.1.4 Economic growth projections (€153 billion by 2020)

An early projection estimated about €153 billion of additional economic benefit by 2020. Figures of this kind come from consulting and association studies and combine productivity gains, new revenue from products and services, and cost savings. They depend strongly on assumptions about how quickly companies adopt the technology, and estimates differ widely between studies.

Because 2020 has passed, present it as an expectation, not an outcome, and compare it with other estimates: Bitkom and Fraunhofer IAO (2014) calculated about €78 billion of extra value added by 2025, and PwC (2014) reported that German industry planned to invest about €40 billion a year in Industrie 4.0 solutions up to 2020.

Example: If a sector with €100 billion of value added raises productivity by 2% a year through digitalisation, after five years it earns roughly €10 billion more per year. This is how such projections are built up.

2.1.5 Positioning Germany as global technology leader

Germany's claim to leadership rests on three pillars. Technology supply: Siemens, Bosch, SAP, Festo, Trumpf and thousands of SMEs deliver machines and automation worldwide. Standards: DIN, DKE and the Platform shaped RAMI 4.0, the Asset Administration Shell and many OPC UA companion specifications. Showcases: Hannover Messe, testbeds such as SmartFactoryKL and flagship plants such as Amberg.

The weak points are lagging digital infrastructure, a small software and cloud sector, bureaucracy and high energy costs. Leadership is therefore contested, as the comparisons in 1.1.14 to 1.1.16 show.

Example: Hannover Messe, the world's largest industrial fair, has used the Industrie 4.0 theme since 2011 to bring buyers and suppliers from around the world to Germany every year.

2.2 Mass Customization and Flexible Production

Industrie 4.0 promises products tailored to each customer at close to mass-production cost. This section explains how production becomes flexible enough to do that.

2.2.1 Individualized production at scale

Mass customisation means producing individually configured products at costs close to those of standard products. It requires a digital configurator for the customer, automatic transfer of the order to engineering and production (the 'digital thread'), flexible machines that need no retooling, and logistics that deliver the right part to the right place. 'Batch size one' is the extreme form. It is attractive where customers will pay for fit or style, such as cars, shoes, furniture and food.

Example: mymuesli from Passau lets customers compose their own muesli online from a long list of ingredients. Each order is mixed individually, but the processes behind it are standardised.

2.2.2 Dynamic production line adaptation

Dynamic adaptation means that a line changes its set-up when the product mix changes, with little downtime. The enablers are quick-change tooling, programmable robots, modular stations on driverless vehicles and software that releases orders in an optimal sequence. Typical measures are changeover time (SMED method) and the range of variants a line can handle without conversion. The aim is to run different products or variants in a mixed sequence.

Example: BMW describes its Leipzig plant as able to build models with combustion, plug-in hybrid and electric drives on the same line, in the order in which customers have ordered them.

2.2.3 Customer-driven manufacturing

In customer-driven manufacturing, production is triggered by customer orders (build-to-order) rather than by forecasts, and customers take part in design through configurators, apps and co-creation. Data from products in use feed back into design, so the product becomes a sensor. The prerequisites are an end-to-end digital order flow, reliable delivery dates and a flexible supply chain. The risk is a 'variant explosion' that raises complexity and cost, so companies set limits on the options offered.

Example: In an online car configurator, a customer chooses colour, engine and equipment. The order goes straight to the plant's scheduling system, and parts are called off from suppliers automatically.

2.2.4 Flexible Manufacturing Systems (FMS)

A flexible manufacturing system is a group of CNC machines connected by automated material handling and a central computer, so that different parts can be produced in any order without manual set-up. The first systems appeared in the late 1960s and 1970s. In Industrie 4.0 they develop further: machines are networked with standard interfaces, and AI helps with scheduling. The trade-off is high investment, which pays off for medium volumes of many different variants.

Example: A machining centre with a pallet store can run unattended through the night, with the control system calling in different workpieces and tools according to the job list.

2.2.5 Modular production concepts

Modular production divides the factory into self-contained modules with standard mechanical, electrical and data interfaces that can be rearranged or added. 'Plug-and-produce' lets a new module announce its capabilities automatically. KUKA's matrix production concept and car-maker pilots replace the rigid assembly chain with free-standing cells served by driverless vehicles. In the process industry the equivalent is the Module Type Package (MTP) approach.

Example: In the SmartFactoryKL demonstrator, modules from different suppliers were connected in varying order, and products moved between them on driverless carriers. Audi has trialled modular assembly where cars move between stations on driverless transport systems.

2.3 Innovation Ecosystem

Germany's innovation system links companies, research organisations, universities and government. The figures below show its scale; the sub-sections describe how it works.

2.3.1 R&D investment (€88.7 billion in 2023)

A reported €88.7 billion of R&D investment in 2023 appears to refer to business-sector spending. Germany’s total R&D intensity is about 3.1% of GDP, above the EU average of roughly 2.2% and above the EU’s 3% target, with the business sector financing about two-thirds. R&D is concentrated in automotive, electrical engineering, machinery and chemicals, the sectors most affected by Industrie 4.0.

The exact amount depends on the definition and the source, so cite the statistics office or Stifterverband for the year you use.

Example: Large firms such as Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, Bosch and Siemens each spend several billion euros a year on R&D, a large part of it on software and electrification.

2.3.2 Patent leadership (25,000+ EPO applications in 2024)

Patent applications at the European Patent Office (EPO) are a common indicator of inventive activity. Germany files more applications than any other European country, roughly 25,000 per year, and is among the top countries worldwide with the USA, Japan, China and South Korea. Patents are strongest in transport, electrical machinery, measurement and mechanical engineering, which matches the Industrie 4.0 supplier base (see 2.6).

A patent count measures activity, not value: quality, citations and licensing matter too.

Example: German companies such as Siemens, Bosch, BMW and Mercedes-Benz regularly appear among the largest applicants at the EPO.

2.3.3 Research institutions and universities

Four non-university research organisations form the backbone: Fraunhofer (applied research, about 75 institutes, partly financed by industry contracts), Max Planck (basic research), Helmholtz (large-scale infrastructure) and Leibniz. The DFKI (German Research Center for Artificial Intelligence) specialises in AI. Technical universities such as RWTH Aachen, TU Munich, KIT and TU Darmstadt supply research and graduates, and DFG-funded clusters such as RWTH's 'Internet of Production' address Industrie 4.0 directly.

Example: Fraunhofer IPA in Stuttgart develops robots and production automation together with companies, and its testbeds are open to SMEs.

2.3.4 Public-private partnerships

Many Industrie 4.0 projects are run jointly by government, industry and research. Examples: the Platform Industrie 4.0; the International Data Spaces Association (founded 2016) for secure data exchange; Catena-X (2021), the automotive data network; and Gaia-X (2019/20) for European data infrastructure. The Fraunhofer model itself is a partnership: a large share of its income comes from industry contracts. The federal 'Research Campus' programme sets up long-term cooperation between science and industry under one roof.

Example: ARENA2036 in Stuttgart brings together car makers, suppliers, the University of Stuttgart and Fraunhofer to develop flexible vehicle production.

2.3.5 Innovation clusters and hubs

Regional clusters concentrate expertise and shorten the path from research to product. Examples: it's OWL in East Westphalia-Lippe (intelligent technical systems, with Phoenix Contact, Beckhoff, Claas, Miele and others); Cyber Valley in Tübingen and Stuttgart for AI; Silicon Saxony around Dresden for microelectronics; the Innovation Park Artificial Intelligence in Heilbronn; and the federal Digital Hub Initiative, which designated regional digital hubs from 2017.

Example: it's OWL was a winner of the federal Leading-Edge Cluster competition and helped Mittelstand firms in the region to build intelligent, networked machines.

2.4 Sustainability and Green Manufacturing

Industrie 4.0 is also meant to make production greener. Data make energy and material flows visible, and visible flows can be optimised.

2.4.1 Energy-efficient production

Industry uses a large share of Germany's final energy, so efficiency is both a cost issue and a climate issue. Industrie 4.0 supports it through machine-level energy monitoring (the basis of ISO 50001 energy management), the detection of standby losses, scheduling that shifts heavy loads to times of cheap or green power, waste-heat recovery and efficient drives. Data make savings visible and verifiable. A caution is the rebound effect: efficiency gains can be cancelled out if output simply grows.

Example: Compressed air is often the most expensive utility in a plant. Sensors that detect leaks and automatic control of compressor pressure can save a noticeable share of its energy use.

2.4.2 Circular economy principles: resource-efficient production, recycling loops

Instead of 'take, make, dispose', a circular economy keeps products and materials in use through durable design, repair, reuse, remanufacturing and recycling. Industrie 4.0 helps with digital product passports (the EU's 2024 Ecodesign Regulation introduces the Digital Product Passport; the Asset Administration Shell is a suitable carrier), tracking of components, take-back logistics and automated quality checks of used parts. Germany's Circular Economy Act (KrWG) and the EU Circular Economy Action Plan provide the legal frame.

Key points

  • Resource-efficient production: less material and energy per unit, less scrap.
  • Recycling loops: material from end-of-life products returns to production, supported by tracking data.
  • Design for circularity: products built for disassembly and repair.

Example: Bosch and Mercedes-Benz run remanufacturing programmes in which used starters, engines or batteries are collected, tested, refurbished and sold again with a new warranty.

2.4.3 Resource optimization

Resource optimisation reduces material, water and consumables per unit of output. Tools are AI-based nesting (cutting layouts that minimise scrap), additive manufacturing that builds near-net-shape parts, process control that avoids off-spec product, and exchange of by-products inside industrial parks. Real-time data show where material is lost, which is the first step to reducing it.

Example: At BASF's Verbund site the waste heat or by-product of one plant is the input of another, so resources are used several times. Laser-cutting software that arranges parts on a sheet to minimise offcuts saves material on every sheet.

2.4.4 Carbon-neutral manufacturing goals: renewable energy, energy-efficient systems

Germany has legislated climate neutrality by 2045 (Federal Climate Change Act, amended 2021), the EU by 2050. For manufacturers this means renewable electricity, electrification of heat, hydrogen in hard-to-abate sectors such as steel and chemicals, and energy-efficient systems. The EU Emissions Trading System puts a price on carbon, which makes the business case. Companies set their own targets: Siemens aims for net zero in its own operations by 2030, and Bosch has reported its sites as carbon neutral since 2020, partly with the help of offsets.

Example: The Salzgitter steelworks' SALCOS project plans to replace coal-based blast furnaces with hydrogen-ready direct-reduction plants and electric arc furnaces to cut CO₂ emissions sharply.

2.4.5 Sustainable supply chains: eco-design, ethical sourcing, carbon tracking

Sustainability does not stop at the factory gate. Eco-design reduces impact at the design stage; ethical sourcing checks human rights and environmental standards at suppliers; carbon tracking calculates the product carbon footprint along the chain. Legal drivers are Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (2023) and the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (adopted 2024 and since softened and delayed). Technologies include digital product passports, supplier portals and Catena-X's standardised exchange of footprint data.

Example: A carmaker asks suppliers three tiers deep for the carbon footprint of aluminium parts, and the data arrive through Catena-X connectors in a common format.

2.5 Future-Ready Business Models

Connected products and data change not only how things are made but also how companies earn money: from selling things to selling outcomes, data and platform access.

2.5.1 Product-as-a-Service (PaaS): selling outcomes instead of products

In Product-as-a-Service the customer pays for the use or outcome of a product instead of buying it: per hour, per piece, per cubic metre. The manufacturer keeps ownership, runs and maintains the product and therefore has an incentive to make it durable, efficient and reliable. It needs connected products (to measure use), flexible billing and financial strength for the asset base.

2.5.2 Data-driven business models

Data-driven models turn information from products and processes into revenue: selling analytics (for example a fleet efficiency report), improving service through remote diagnostics, using data for better product design, or selling data itself in data marketplaces. Success depends on clear data rights in contracts, trust between partners and sufficient data quality. The EU Data Act (applicable since September 2025) gives users of connected products rights over the data they generate, which affects such models.

Example: The Munich start-up KONUX analyses sensor data from rail switches for railway operators, and Lufthansa Technik's AVIATAR sells predictive-maintenance analytics for aircraft fleets.

2.5.3 Platform-based ecosystems

A platform connects different groups (machine builders, software developers, customers) and gains value as more join (network effects). In industry examples are Siemens Xcelerator, SAP's Business Technology Platform and Bosch's IoT Suite and Nexeed. German machine builders also founded joint platforms, such as ADAMOS (2017, by DMG Mori, Dürr, Software AG and Zeiss). Platforms are hard for a company that has always sold hardware, because they require openness to competitors' products.

Example: On the Siemens Xcelerator Marketplace, partners offer apps and services next to Siemens products, and customers choose from a common catalogue.

2.5.4 Servitization in manufacturing

Servitization is the shift of manufacturers from selling goods only to selling goods bundled with services: installation, training, remote monitoring, predictive maintenance, upgrades and financing. Services often have higher margins and steadier revenue than product sales, and they bind customers more closely. The challenge is organisational: a company that is good at engineering must build service sales, pricing and support.

Example: A machine tool maker sells a five-year contract that includes guaranteed availability. The maker's monitoring system schedules maintenance, and the customer pays a monthly fee.

2.5.5 New revenue streams from digital services

Connected products can earn money after the sale: software upgrades, feature activation on demand, subscriptions to analytics, digital twins as a service, marketplaces and pay-per-use. Carmakers offer over-the-air updates and paid functions; machine builders sell apps for their controls. Customer acceptance is the issue. BMW's plan to sell heated seats by subscription in 2022 drew heavy criticism, and the company later moved away from monthly subscriptions for such hardware-based features.

Example: A packaging-machine maker offers a paid software module that automatically optimises changeover settings, delivered as an update to machines already in the field.

2.5.A Mass customization: personalised products at mass-production cost

Seen as a business model, mass customisation moves value from the product to the configuration experience. Customers pay a premium for a product that fits them, the company gains data about preferences, and made-to-order production reduces unsold stock. The model works where the factory can handle many variants (see 2.2) and where the configurator is easy to use. Its limits are lead time and the cost of complexity.

Example: Adidas' Speedfactory (see 5.5.3) tried to make customised shoes close to the customer. The idea was attractive, but the economics did not work at scale, which is a useful counter-example.

2.5.B Collaborative platforms: shared manufacturing resources and digital marketplaces

Collaborative platforms let companies share production capacity, data or knowledge. Manufacturing-as-a-service marketplaces match customers' designs with free capacity at contract manufacturers; data spaces such as Catena-X let companies share data under agreed rules. Benefits are better utilisation of machines, faster access to capacity and resilience when a supplier fails. Challenges include IP protection, quality assurance and trust.

Example: Marketplaces such as Xometry and Protolabs let an engineer upload a CAD file and get a quote and delivery of a machined or 3D-printed part from a network of manufacturers within days.

2.6 Key Technology Sectors

Five sectors carry much of Germany’s industrial innovation. Patent counts vary by technology field, classification and reporting year, so each figure should be interpreted in the context of the source and category used.

2.6.1 Automotive industry transformation

The automotive industry is Germany's largest industrial sector, with roughly three-quarters of a million direct jobs. It is changing in three ways at once: electrification (batteries and electric drives), software-defined vehicles and digital production. The shift is painful: electric cars need fewer parts, new competitors such as Tesla and Chinese makers are fast, and several German plants are being downsized. At the same time the sector pioneers Industrie 4.0 in production and supply-chain data (Catena-X).

Example: Volkswagen converted its Zwickau plant to all-electric production in 2019–20. Tesla's Gigafactory Berlin-Brandenburg shows a different model, with large single-piece castings that simplify body assembly.

2.6.2 Mechanical engineering (11,153 patents in transport)

Mechanical and plant engineering is the heart of the Mittelstand: machine tools, packaging and printing machines, handling and robotics, pumps and drives. The VDMA association represents mainly family-owned SMEs. The figure of 11,153 patent applications refers to transport, which the EPO classifies separately from mechanical engineering, so the technology field should be named when quoting it. The sector is both a supplier of Industrie 4.0 technology and one of its biggest users.

Example: Trumpf (laser machine tools), DMG Mori (machining centres) and Voith (drive technology) all add sensors, software and services to their machines.

2.6.3 Electrical engineering (8,248 patents)

Electrical engineering covers automation and drive technology, power electronics, energy technology, electronics and semiconductors. Siemens, Bosch, Infineon and many specialised firms (represented by the ZVEI association) belong here. The sector provides the controllers, drives and sensors that give factories their 'nervous system' and the power electronics for electric vehicles and renewable energy.

Example: Dresden's Silicon Saxony cluster hosts chip makers such as Infineon and GlobalFoundries, and a new fab for automotive and industrial chips (ESMC) is under construction there.

2.6.4 Measurement technology (4,712 patents)

Without measurement there is no data, so measurement and sensor technology is the foundation of the smart factory. Germany has a strong tradition here: Zeiss in optics and metrology, SICK in industrial sensors and LiDAR safety scanners, Pepperl+Fuchs, ifm and many others, supported by the national metrology institute PTB. Trends are smarter sensors with built-in processing, IO-Link communication, and inline measurement in production.

Example: SICK's safety laser scanners let driverless vehicles and robots detect people and slow down or stop, which makes close human-machine cooperation possible.

2.6.5 Computer technology integration

Software and information technology are the connecting layer: enterprise software (SAP), industrial software (Siemens), embedded systems, cloud and AI. The main task is IT/OT convergence, the merging of office IT with operational technology on the shop floor, which brings different cultures, security needs and life cycles together. German strengths are enterprise software and embedded systems; weaknesses are consumer platforms and the scale of cloud providers, which is why data sovereignty initiatives matter.

Example: An SAP ERP system sends a production order to the MES, which controls machines on the shop floor, and confirmations flow back so that stock and costs are updated in real time.

2.7 Future Technologies

Four technologies are expected to shape the next stage of the smart factory: 5G, edge computing, blockchain and quantum computing.

2.7.1 5G in manufacturing: ultra-low latency for real-time machine communication

Beyond today's pilots, 5G is expected to become a standard industrial network. Release 16 and later versions of the standard add features for industry: deterministic low latency, time synchronisation, network slicing (separate virtual networks for different needs) and positioning. With private campus networks, a factory can connect mobile robots, wireless tools and sensors without cables and reconfigure lines quickly. The industry body 5G-ACIA, set up in 2018 under the ZVEI, works on industrial requirements.

Example: A mobile robot that receives its safety-relevant commands over 5G with a latency of a few milliseconds can move freely between production areas without a wired connection.

2.7.2 Edge computing: processing data at the source rather than in the cloud

Looking forward, edge computing will host AI models close to machines (edge AI) and make small devices intelligent (TinyML). Techniques such as federated learning train a shared model on data from several plants without moving the raw data, which protects trade secrets. Containers and orchestration, as in cloud computing, make it easier to deploy and update applications on large numbers of edge devices.

Example: Siemens' Industrial Edge lets a plant run a trained quality-inspection model on a device next to the line and update it centrally for many sites.

2.7.3 Blockchain in supply chains: transparency, traceability and smart contracts

A blockchain is a shared, tamper-evident ledger. In supply chains it can record the origin and handling of goods, so that all parties see the same history, and smart contracts can trigger payment automatically when conditions are met. Promising uses are provenance of raw materials such as cobalt, certificates of origin and carbon accounting. The limits are real: scalability, cost, the need to get all parties to join, and the fact that a blockchain cannot verify the truth of what is entered at the start. Several large projects, such as Maersk's TradeLens, have ended.

Example: BMW's PartChain project uses a blockchain to trace parts through the supply chain, and Volvo has used the tracing firm Circulor for cobalt in batteries.

2.7.4 Quantum computing: optimization of complex logistics and production planning

Quantum computers use quantum-mechanical effects to solve certain problems faster than classical machines. Possible industrial uses are combinatorial optimisation (routing, scheduling), the simulation of materials and chemistry, and machine-learning tasks. Today's machines are small and noisy, so a practical advantage in industry has not yet been proven. Germany invests in the field: a national framework programme for quantum technologies, and IBM's Quantum System One at Fraunhofer in Ehningen (2021), the first in Europe.

Example: Volkswagen tested quantum algorithms for traffic flow, and BMW has run quantum-computing challenges on problems such as supply-chain and materials optimisation.

2.8 Future Workforce

People remain central to the smart factory, but their tasks change: they work beside machines, use digital tools and need new skills throughout their careers.

2.8.1 Human-machine collaboration: cobots (collaborative robots) working alongside humans

Cobots are robots designed to work next to people without protective fences. Safety is defined in the standards ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066, which describe four modes: safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, and power and force limiting. The robot takes over heavy, repetitive or precise tasks, and the person contributes judgement and dexterity. Acceptance depends on trust, training and good ergonomics.

Example: Bosch's APAS assistant and KUKA's LBR iiwa are German cobots used for assembly tasks such as inserting parts, and exoskeletons from companies such as Ottobock support workers at overhead tasks in car plants.

2.8.2 Augmented Reality (AR) and Virtual Reality (VR): training, maintenance and remote assistance

AR overlays digital information on the real view (through smart glasses or tablets); VR immerses the user in a simulated environment. In industry AR shows step-by-step assembly or repair instructions and lets an expert see what a technician sees. VR is used for safe training, design reviews and factory planning. Benefits are fewer errors, faster learning and reduced travel; obstacles are hardware comfort and content creation costs.

Example: Porsche's 'Tech Live Look' lets a workshop technician share a view with a remote expert through smart glasses, and thyssenkrupp equipped elevator technicians with Microsoft HoloLens.

2.8.3 Reskilling and upskilling: preparing workers for the digital factory

Upskilling deepens existing skills (for example a machine operator learns to read data dashboards); reskilling prepares for a new role (a fitter becomes an automation technician). The World Economic Forum's 2023 report estimated that a large share of workers' core skills will change within five years. In Germany, the Qualification Opportunities Act (2019) and the National Skills Strategy support company training, with the Federal Employment Agency co-funding courses.

Example: At Bosch's Blaichach plant about 3,000 employees were trained for the networked factory while the plant was being converted, so that the new technology came with people who could use it.

2.9 Vision 2030 and Beyond

The long-term vision includes highly autonomous factories, supply chains that can withstand shocks and common global standards.

2.9.1 Fully autonomous factories: lights-out manufacturing

A 'lights-out' factory runs without people on the shop floor, so lights and heating are not needed. Japanese robot maker Fanuc has long operated production lines that run unattended for extended periods, and Philips built a shaver factory in the Netherlands with 128 robots and a handful of people. In Germany, full autonomy is limited to some production steps. The obstacles are variable parts, complex maintenance and the economics: lights-out pays only at high volumes of stable products. Many experts see human-robot teams, not empty factories, as the realistic goal for 2030.

Example: A machining cell with automatic pallet loading and tool changing can run unattended overnight and at weekends, while staff monitor it remotely.

2.9.2 Resilient supply chains: post-pandemic learnings and nearshoring trends

The pandemic, the 2021 chip shortage, the Suez Canal blockage and the 2022 energy crisis showed how fragile just-in-time chains are. Responses are dual sourcing, larger buffers for critical parts, nearshoring or 'friend-shoring', real-time visibility and digital twins of supply networks. The EU Chips Act (2023) aims to double Europe's share of chip production, and Germany's 2023 China strategy speaks of 'de-risking' not decoupling. Resilience costs money, so companies weigh it against efficiency.

Example: In 2021 German carmakers had to cut production for weeks because they lacked semiconductors, which led several of them to sign direct supply agreements with chip makers.

2.9.3 Global standards: international interoperability norms

Industrie 4.0 only scales if standards are shared across borders. Germany promotes its architectures in ISO, IEC and ITU committees: RAMI 4.0 (published as DIN SPEC 91345), the Asset Administration Shell (IEC 63278) and OPC UA (IEC 62541). The Industrial Digital Twin Association (IDTA, founded 2020) develops AAS submodels. Cooperation with Japan, the USA, France and others, and the Standardization Council Industrie 4.0, helps to avoid separate regional standards.

Example: Because the Asset Administration Shell is now an IEC standard, a supplier in any country can deliver the same digital nameplate and technical data for a component.

3.1 Evolution of Manufacturing Intelligence

This section traces how production moved from machines that only follow instructions to systems that learn, and where artificial intelligence fits.

3.1.1 From mechanization to digitalization

Each stage of industrialisation automated a different human capacity. Mechanisation replaced muscle power; electrification and automation replaced repetitive control; digitalisation replaces routine information processing, such as recording, calculating and transmitting data; and the intelligence stage adds perception, prediction and decisions. Digitalisation is the bridge: only when processes are captured as data can algorithms analyse and improve them. Many SMEs are still working on this bridge, for example by replacing paper job cards with digital orders.

Example: The Jacquard loom (1804) used punched cards to control patterns, an early step from mechanisation to programmable control that later inspired computing. Today, an ML model that spots defects in a fabric image continues the same line.

3.1.2 The role of artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) is software that performs tasks that normally require human intelligence: perceiving (images, sound), predicting, optimising and, with generative AI, producing text, code or designs. In manufacturing, AI is used wherever data are plentiful and rules are hard to write by hand: quality inspection, maintenance, scheduling, energy management and robot control. Generative AI adds natural-language assistants for engineers and operators. AI does not replace the physical process; it improves decisions around it, and it must be validated, monitored and governed.

Example: Siemens and Microsoft launched the 'Industrial Copilot' (announced 2023), a generative-AI assistant that helps engineers create automation code and troubleshoot machines with natural-language questions.

3.1.3 Machine learning in production

Machine learning (ML) lets software learn patterns from data instead of being programmed with rules. Supervised learning uses labelled examples (for instance images marked 'good' or 'defective'); unsupervised learning finds structure or anomalies without labels; reinforcement learning learns by trial and reward, for example in robot control or scheduling. Production use requires more than a model: clean data, labelling, deployment on edge devices, monitoring of model drift and retraining (MLOps). Often the data work takes more effort than the algorithm.

Example: A supervised model trained on 20,000 labelled images of cast parts classifies each new part as good, porous or cracked within milliseconds, and sends doubtful cases to a human inspector.

3.1.4 Cognitive manufacturing systems

Cognitive systems go beyond single AI functions. They combine perception (sensors, vision), knowledge (knowledge graphs, ontologies, digital twins), reasoning and learning, and can explain what they do and adapt to new situations. The aim is a plant that 'understands' its state and goals, for example by connecting maintenance records, engineering data and live sensor values to propose a diagnosis. Today most deployments are partial: assistants and decision support rather than fully cognitive factories.

Example: A maintenance assistant reads equipment manuals, past repair reports and current alarms, and answers a technician's question in plain language with the likely cause and the procedure.

3.2 Artificial Intelligence in Industry

A closer look at the AI methods used on the shop floor and in planning, alongside market research on the sector’s growth.

3.2.1 AI market growth ($10.74 billion in 2023 to $30.83 billion by 2032)

One market-research projection places the AI market at $10.74 billion in 2023 and $30.83 billion by 2032. That implies compound annual growth of about 12.4% (30.83 / 10.74 = 2.87, which over nine years is 12.4% a year). The scope of such figures differs (global or national, all AI or AI in manufacturing only, software only or including hardware and services), so the research firm and scope should be named. The direction is consistent across studies: strong double-digit growth driven by quality inspection, predictive maintenance and generative AI.

Example: If a market is worth $10.74 billion in 2023 and grows 12.4% a year, it is about $12.1 billion in 2024, $15.3 billion in 2026 and reaches roughly $30.8 billion in 2032.

3.2.2 Machine learning algorithms for quality control

Quality control uses ML in two ways. Classification (random forests, support vector machines, convolutional neural networks) learns to separate good from bad parts using examples. Anomaly detection learns what normal looks like and flags deviations, which suits very low defect rates where bad examples are rare. Evaluation uses precision (how many alarms are real) and recall (how many defects are found). The cost of a false alarm and of a missed defect usually differ, so the threshold is set by business impact.

Example: In a plant with only 11 defects per million units, there are almost no defect images to learn from, so an unsupervised model learns the pattern of normal test curves and flags any unusual curve for inspection.

3.2.3 AI-powered predictive analytics

Predictive analytics uses historical and live data to forecast outcomes: a machine's remaining life, next week's demand, the probability that a batch fails quality control, or the delivery delay of a shipment. Methods include time-series forecasting, regression, gradient-boosted trees and neural networks. The forecasts are only useful when linked to action: an automatic maintenance order, a changed production plan or a procurement trigger.

Example: The online retailer Otto uses ML demand forecasts to decide in advance how much stock to order, which reduces both unsold goods and delivery delays.

3.2.4 Computer vision for inspection: AI-powered defect detection and visual inspection

Computer vision uses cameras and deep-learning models (typically convolutional neural networks) to inspect products faster and more consistently than people. It finds scratches, dents, missing parts, soldering faults or wrong labels, and it can read codes and measure dimensions. Success depends on lighting, camera set-up and a representative training set, and the model usually runs at the edge to meet line speed. Synthetic images can supplement scarce defect data.

Example: A camera above a conveyor captures an image of each part. A neural network trained on thousands of labelled images flags scratches, and the part is rejected by an air jet. Machine-vision software such as MVTec's HALCON (from Munich) and cameras from Basler are widely used.

3.2.5 Natural language processing in manufacturing: voice-controlled interfaces and documentation

Natural language processing (NLP) lets people talk to systems and lets systems read text. Uses are voice control for hands-busy workers, search in manuals and technical documents, automatic analysis of maintenance reports and customer complaints, translation of work instructions into several languages, and, with large language models, the drafting of documents and code. Risks are wrong answers ('hallucinations') and leakage of confidential data, so companies use controlled, company-specific knowledge sources.

Example: A technician asks a mobile assistant, 'Why is press 4 showing error E27?' and receives the cause and fix from the manufacturer's manual and past service cases. SAP's Joule and Siemens' Industrial Copilot are examples of such assistants.

3.2.A Machine learning for predictive maintenance: anticipating equipment failures before they happen

Predictive maintenance models use two main approaches. Anomaly-based models learn normal behaviour and detect drift; prognostic models estimate the remaining useful life using degradation patterns. Typical inputs are vibration spectra (analysed with the Fourier transform), temperature, pressure, motor current and operating hours. The practical problems are few examples of real failures, differing machines and noisy data. Successful projects begin with a few critical assets, define the cost of downtime and link alerts to a maintenance workflow.

Example: Schaeffler's OPTIME system uses small wireless vibration sensors on ordinary machines and a cloud service that alerts when a bearing or gearbox shows signs of damage.

3.3 Automation and Robotics

Robots are moving from caged, fixed tasks to flexible, collaborative and mobile roles. This section covers the main robot types and the trends in robotics.

3.3.1 Collaborative robots (cobots)

Cobots are robots built to share a workspace with people. Typical features are lightweight arms, force and torque sensing, rounded surfaces, speed limits and easy programming by hand-guiding or graphical interfaces. They are cheaper and faster to deploy than classic industrial robots, which makes them attractive for SMEs with small batches. Their payload and speed are lower, and the risk assessment of the whole application, not just the robot, decides safety.

Example: Universal Robots (Denmark) leads the market, while KUKA's LBR iiwa and Bosch's APAS are German examples used for tasks such as screwing, inserting and testing.

3.3.2 Service robots in assembly

In the International Federation of Robotics' definition, service robots perform useful tasks for humans outside industrial automation, but the term also covers mobile assistants that support industrial work: delivering parts, holding workpieces, handing tools to workers. Combined with a mobile base and a robot arm they form mobile manipulators that can serve several stations. Their appeal is flexibility; their challenge is reliable perception in busy, changing environments.

Example: NEURA Robotics from Metzingen builds 'cognitive' robots with integrated sensors that can recognise objects and people, and a mobile manipulator can load and unload several machines in a hall.

3.3.3 Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs)

AMRs navigate on their own using LiDAR, cameras and a map (SLAM, simultaneous localisation and mapping). Unlike AGVs they need no wires or tape and drive around obstacles. A fleet manager assigns transport jobs and coordinates traffic, ideally through an open interface such as VDA 5050. AMRs suit changing layouts and mixed human traffic. Limits are floor conditions, battery management and the interplay with safety rules.

Example: idealworks, a BMW subsidiary, markets the iw.hub AMR for transporting parts between production areas, and BMW uses AMRs in its plants.

3.3.4 Robotic Process Automation (RPA)

RPA uses software 'bots' that imitate human clicks and keystrokes to automate rule-based office tasks, such as entering orders, matching invoices or copying data between systems. It is not a physical robot, but it belongs to the automation picture because it closes gaps between ERP, MES and other systems that lack interfaces. RPA combined with process mining, which uses event logs to find inefficiencies (the Munich company Celonis is a leader), identifies what is worth automating. Fragile bots break when screens change, so they are a bridge to proper integration, not the final goal.

Example: A bot reads incoming purchase-order emails, enters the data in the ERP system and sends a confirmation, saving staff hours each day.

3.3.5 Human-robot collaboration

Human-robot collaboration ranges from coexistence (shared space, separate tasks) through cooperation (shared task, taking turns) to true collaboration (working together on the same part at the same time). The safety standards ISO 10218 (revised in 2025) and ISO/TS 15066 limit speeds and contact forces. Good design assigns tasks by strengths: the robot lifts, positions and repeats; the person judges, adapts and handles exceptions. Ergonomics, trust and works-council agreements are as important as the technology.

Example: BMW has used collaborative robots on assembly lines where the robot holds or fits a heavy component and the worker completes the sensitive final connection.

3.3.A From fixed automation to adaptive robots

Classic robots repeat programmed motions and fail when a part is placed slightly differently. Adaptive robots perceive and adjust: vision-guided picking, force-torque control for assembly, learning by demonstration, skill-based programming and AI models for grasping. This allows robots to handle variation, shorter batches and quick changeovers without a programmer for each task. Reliability, cycle time and safety certification of learning systems are still being solved.

Example: Siemens' robot-picking software uses a 3D camera and AI so that a standard robot can pick randomly piled parts from a bin without being programmed for each part type.

3.3.B Swarm robotics: coordinated multi-robot systems

Swarm robotics takes inspiration from insects: many simple robots coordinate through local rules, without central control, to achieve a joint result. Benefits are scalability, robustness (if one unit fails, others continue) and flexibility. In logistics, groups of small vehicles can share loads or form conveyor lines. Hurdles are communication, safety and predictability.

Example: Fraunhofer IML's 'LoadRunner' vehicles form a cellular transport system, in which many small vehicles organise themselves to move goods in a warehouse. Festo's BionicANTs demonstrate cooperative behaviour in the laboratory.

3.3.C Soft robotics: flexible robots for delicate handling tasks

Soft robots are made of compliant materials such as silicone and use air pressure or flexible actuators. They adapt their shape to an object and handle fragile or irregular items gently: fruit, baked goods, glass, electronics. They are also inherently safer around people. Limits are lower force, precision and durability compared with rigid robots, and difficulties in modelling and control.

Example: Festo developed the OctopusGripper, a soft silicone gripper inspired by octopus tentacles, and the BionicSoftArm; soft grippers of this kind are used in food packing to pick up delicate items.

3.4 Smart Sensors and Data Acquisition

Sensors and embedded devices are the eyes and nerves of the smart factory. This section covers how data are captured, combined and used.

3.4.1 IoT sensors and actuators

Sensors measure physical quantities (position, temperature, pressure, vibration, flow, images); actuators act (valves, motors, grippers). Smart versions contain a small processor, can be configured remotely and report their health. The IO-Link standard (IEC 61131-9) connects such devices digitally to the controller and lets them transmit parameters and diagnostics, not only a switching signal. Wireless sensors and energy harvesting ease retrofitting of old machines.

Example: Festo's Motion Terminal combines valves and sensors and can change its function through software 'apps', for example switching from a gripping to a clamping profile without hardware change.

3.4.2 Embedded systems

An embedded system is a computer built into a machine or device: a microcontroller or system-on-chip with firmware, often with a real-time operating system. It must be reliable for years, work within tight power and cost limits and, for safety functions, comply with standards such as IEC 61508. As devices become smarter and connected, their software grows, and with it the need for secure updates and, in the EU, compliance with the Cyber Resilience Act.

Example: A drive controller contains an embedded processor that regulates motor current thousands of times per second and also reports its temperature and operating hours to the plant network.

3.4.3 Real-time data collection and real-time analytics

Data collection moves signals from sensors into storage and analysis. Machine data are collected through protocols such as OPC UA and MQTT, buffered at the edge, and stored in time-series databases. Real-time analytics evaluate the stream as it arrives, with instant insights from the production floor: live OEE, drift alarms and dashboards. The key design decisions are sampling rate, time synchronisation, data quality checks and which data to keep, since storing everything is costly.

Example: At Siemens' Amberg plant, tens of millions of data points per day from machines and products are evaluated continuously, so that deviations show up in minutes rather than at the end of a shift.

3.4.4 Sensor fusion technologies

Sensor fusion combines data from several sensors to obtain a more accurate, complete or robust picture than any one sensor could give. Methods range from simple averaging to Kalman filters and neural networks. Typical combinations are camera plus LiDAR plus inertial sensors for robot navigation, or vision plus force for assembly. Fusion improves accuracy and keeps working when one sensor fails or is disturbed.

Example: An AMR combines wheel odometry, an inertial sensor and a LiDAR scan to know its position within a few centimetres, even when one source is momentarily unreliable.

3.4.5 Condition monitoring systems

Condition monitoring continuously measures the health of machines to detect wear or faults early. Common techniques are vibration analysis, thermography, oil analysis, acoustic emission and motor current analysis. Standards such as ISO 10816/20816 (vibration severity) and ISO 13374 (data processing) structure this. It is the sensor basis of predictive maintenance: monitoring detects, analytics predict, and maintenance acts.

Example: Wind turbines are equipped with vibration sensors on gearboxes and bearings, and the data are analysed remotely so that repairs are scheduled for calm weather.

3.4.A Anomaly detection: identifying irregularities in processes

Anomaly detection finds data patterns that deviate from the normal. Methods include statistical limits (control charts), clustering, isolation forests and autoencoder neural networks. It is valuable when failure examples are scarce, and it can monitor processes, machines and also networks for cyber-attacks. The balance is between sensitivity and false alarms: too many alarms are ignored. A good system also shows why something is anomalous.

Example: An autoencoder trained on normal pressure curves of an injection-moulding machine flags a cycle whose curve has a small unusual dip, which later turns out to be a worn check valve.

3.4.B Prescriptive analytics: AI recommending optimal actions

Prescriptive analytics goes beyond forecasting to recommend or trigger the best action, combining predictions with optimisation and simulation under constraints. It answers: 'What should we do?' For example, it can propose the best machine settings, the cheapest time to service a machine or the best production sequence. It needs trust: people must understand and be able to override the recommendation.

Example: Where a predictive model warns that a machine will fail in about ten days, a prescriptive system also proposes the best maintenance window, taking the order book and spare-parts availability into account.

Data only create value when they support decisions. This section covers the types of analytics, the systems that deliver them and the AI-based extensions.

3.5.1 Descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive analytics

The four types of analytics form a maturity ladder, from understanding the past to steering the future. Most companies are strong in descriptive analytics and are building up diagnostic and predictive capabilities; prescriptive use is still rare. Each step needs better data, more skills and more trust in models.

3.5.2 Business intelligence systems

Business intelligence (BI) systems collect data from many sources into a data warehouse and present them as reports, dashboards and ad-hoc analyses. The process runs: extract-transform-load (ETL), model the data, visualise. Tools include SAP Analytics Cloud, Microsoft Power BI and Tableau. In manufacturing, BI ties production data to financial results, for example cost per unit and margin per product. Good BI rests on shared definitions of key figures.

Example: A plant manager's BI dashboard shows output, scrap, energy per unit and delivery performance for each line and lets the manager drill from the plant total down to a single shift.

3.5.3 Decision Support Systems (DSS)

A decision support system helps people make complex decisions through data, models and interactive tools. Types are data-driven (queries, reports), model-driven (simulation, optimisation) and knowledge-driven (rules, expert knowledge). Advanced planning and scheduling (APS) software is a typical manufacturing DSS. Its strength is the 'what-if' analysis: the planner can test options and see the impact before deciding.

Example: A production planner tests what happens if a rush order of 500 units is inserted. The system shows which other orders will be late and proposes a new sequence.

3.5.4 Optimization algorithms

Optimisation algorithms find the best solution under constraints. Methods include linear and mixed-integer programming, constraint programming, metaheuristics (genetic algorithms, simulated annealing) and, increasingly, reinforcement learning. Typical industrial problems are production scheduling, cutting and packing, vehicle routing and network design. Many such problems are NP-hard, so practical tools find good solutions quickly rather than proving the perfect one.

Example: UPS's ORION routing system optimises delivery routes for tens of thousands of drivers. In factories, solvers such as Google's OR-Tools or commercial APS tools do the same for job sequencing.

3.5.5 Performance management dashboards

Dashboards display key performance indicators (KPIs) such as OEE, quality, delivery performance, energy and safety. Good practice is to show few, relevant KPIs, use clear colour codes (green, amber, red), allow drill-down and tailor views by role: operator, supervisor, plant manager. Dashboards at the line (andon boards) drive daily shop-floor meetings. Poor dashboards overload users with numbers without telling them what to do.

Example: A large screen at the end of a line shows planned versus actual output hour by hour, the current OEE and the top three reasons for stops, and the team reviews it at each shift change.

3.5.A Autonomous systems: self-optimizing production lines

Autonomous production lines combine self-optimisation (3.3.1 and 1.3.1) with automatic planning and material flow: orders are released, machines are set up, material is moved and quality is checked with little human intervention. People define goals and handle exceptions. Today this exists in parts, such as automated warehouse-to-line supply or adaptive process control, rather than as a complete system.

Example: The SmartFactoryKL 'Production Level 4' demonstrator showed a line configured automatically by the order: modules, transport and products organised themselves.

3.5.B Digital twins and simulation: virtual replicas for testing and optimization

Beyond the definitions in 1.3.5, simulation provides the main benefit of twins: testing without risk. Discrete-event simulation checks throughput and bottlenecks of a planned line; physics-based models optimise a process; virtual commissioning tests the control software against a virtual machine before the real one exists, shortening start-up time. When live data flow into the twin it can support operation, for example by forecasting the effect of a disturbance.

Example: Before a new assembly line is built, planners run the line in simulation, find that one station limits output, and add a second station on paper rather than after installation.

3.5.C AI-driven supply chain optimization: demand forecasting and inventory management

AI improves supply-chain planning by forecasting demand from many signals (sales history, promotions, weather, economic indicators), setting inventory levels and safety stocks dynamically, and re-planning when disruptions occur. Software such as SAP Integrated Business Planning and Blue Yonder (founded in Karlsruhe) offer these functions. The benefits are lower stock with better availability. A prerequisite is shared, accurate data across the chain.

Example: A supplier of household goods uses ML forecasts at product-and-store level to reduce stock by double-digit percentages while keeping shelves full. Otto's forecasting is a German example.

3.6 Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS)

Section 1.1.10 introduced CPS. Here the focus is on how they are built: the integration of the physical and digital, the embedded computers, the networks, the smart products and self-configuration.

3.6.1 Integration of physical and digital worlds

In a CPS the physical process and its digital representation are linked in both directions: sensors feed the digital model, and decisions from the model act on the process. The '5C architecture' describes the levels: Connection (sensors and data), Conversion (data to information), Cyber (the networked digital twin), Cognition (diagnosis and decisions) and Configuration (self-adjustment). Reliable timing and a consistent data model are essential, otherwise the digital and physical worlds drift apart.

Example: A smart machine tool measures its own vibration (connection), converts it into a stability measure (conversion), compares it with other machines (cyber), recognises chatter (cognition) and adjusts spindle speed (configuration).

3.6.2 Embedded computing in machinery

Machines contain layers of computing: PLCs and motion controllers for deterministic control, industrial PCs for vision and analytics, and microcontrollers inside sensors and drives. A trend is software-defined automation, where control programs run as software on standard hardware, even virtualised, and can be updated without replacing devices. German companies such as Beckhoff (TwinCAT, PC-based control) and Siemens (SIMATIC) are leaders. Real-time behaviour and cybersecurity are the main design constraints.

Example: Beckhoff's XTS system uses linear motors to move many independent carriers along a track, controlled in real time by PC-based software, so products can be routed flexibly between stations.

3.6.3 Networked control systems

In networked control systems, controllers, sensors and actuators communicate through a network rather than through dedicated wires. This brings flexibility and diagnostics but also delay, jitter and the risk of lost messages, which control designers must handle. Industrial Ethernet standards (PROFINET, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP) and Time-Sensitive Networking provide deterministic behaviour, and OPC UA over TSN is the path to unified, vendor-independent communication.

Example: PROFINET, supported by PI in Karlsruhe, is the most widely used industrial Ethernet standard in Europe and connects controllers, drives and I/O devices in many German plants.

3.6.4 Smart products and components

A smart product carries identity and information about itself: a code, an RFID tag or an embedded memory with its recipe, history and requirements. In 'the product steers its own production' the workpiece tells each station what to do. After delivery, smart components with sensors report their use and condition. They enable traceability, individualised processing and new services.

Example: Schaeffler's bearings with built-in sensors report load and temperature during operation. In the SmartFactoryKL demonstrator, each product carries its own memory and decides its way through the production modules.

3.6.5 Self-configuring systems

Self-configuring systems adapt their set-up automatically when conditions change or when a component is added. They rely on machine-readable descriptions of capabilities (OPC UA information models, the Asset Administration Shell) and on negotiation between components. The result is plug-and-produce: a new module is connected and becomes usable without manual engineering. This reduces commissioning time and makes reconfiguration routine.

Example: In the 2018 SmartFactoryKL demonstration, modules from many different companies were exchanged and re-arranged in the line, with the system recognising each module and configuring itself.

3.8 Ethical and Technical Challenges

AI in industry raises questions of fairness, transparency and protection of data. They are also legal topics under the EU AI Act, GDPR and the Data Act.

3.8.1 AI bias in industrial systems

Bias arises when an AI system performs worse or unfairly for some cases or groups. In industry the typical causes are unrepresentative training data (one shift, one supplier, one lighting condition), wrong labels and feedback loops. Bias in technical systems leads to missed defects; in systems that evaluate people, such as productivity scoring or recruitment tools, it can disadvantage workers. Under the EU AI Act, AI in employment is a high-risk use. Countermeasures are diverse data, testing on subgroups, monitoring for drift and human review.

Example: A vision model trained on day-shift images misses defects at night because the lighting differs. A scoring system that rates operators by speed penalises those who work on older machines.

3.8.2 Explainable AI (XAI): making AI decisions transparent

Explainable AI makes the reasons for a model's output understandable. Techniques include feature-importance methods (SHAP, LIME), heat maps showing which image regions led to a decision (Grad-CAM), and simple surrogate rules. Explanations build operator trust, help engineers find faults in the model and support safety certification and the transparency and human-oversight duties of the AI Act. There is often a trade-off between accuracy and explainability.

Example: When a vision system rejects a weld, it overlays a heat map on the image showing the seam region that caused the decision, so the inspector can check it in seconds.

3.8.3 Data privacy and security: protecting proprietary industrial data

Industrial data are valuable (process know-how, product designs) and sometimes personal (operator identities, camera images). The GDPR applies to personal data, the EU Data Act (from September 2025) regulates access to data from connected products, and the trade-secrets law protects confidential know-how. Technical protection includes encryption, access control, anonymisation and, for sharing between companies, sovereign data spaces with usage control (the International Data Spaces and Gaia-X approach). Federated learning allows AI training without exchanging raw data.

Example: Several suppliers want to improve a shared predictive-maintenance model without revealing their production data. With federated learning each trains locally, and only model updates are shared.

4.1 Education and Workforce Transformation

Technology changes only what people can use. This section covers how industrial jobs, roles, working conditions and training systems are changing.

4.1.1 Changing nature of industrial jobs

Industrie 4.0 shifts work from manual, repetitive tasks to supervising, configuring and improving automated systems. Routine jobs are the most exposed, while demand grows for roles that combine technical and data skills. The Institute for Employment Research (IAB) estimates that roughly a third of German jobs could, in technical terms, be largely automated, but technical potential is not the same as actual job loss, because adoption depends on cost, regulation and acceptance, and new tasks appear. The overall picture is change of tasks inside jobs more than disappearance of jobs.

Example: An operator of a manually fed press now supervises a robot-fed cell, reads status dashboards and handles set-ups and faults, which needs more technical knowledge than the old job.

4.1.2 New job roles and competencies

New and changed roles include data analyst, IoT specialist, automation and mechatronics engineer, robot technician, cybersecurity specialist and digital factory manager. Required competencies are digital skills (data literacy, software handling), technical skills across disciplines (mechanics, electronics, IT), and soft skills such as problem solving, collaboration and willingness to keep learning. Employers speak of 'T-shaped' profiles: deep expertise in one area and broad understanding of the others. Since 2018 the German training regulations for metal and electrical occupations include digital content and optional additional qualifications, for example in additive manufacturing and IT security.

Example: A mechatronics technician (an apprenticeship profile that combines mechanical, electrical and software skills) commissions a new cell, reads PLC diagnostics and adjusts sensor parameters through a tablet.

4.1.3 Human-machine collaboration

From the workforce perspective, human-machine collaboration raises the questions of who does what, who decides and who is responsible. Good design lets machines do what they do best (strength, precision, endurance, data processing) and people do what they do best (judgement, flexibility, creativity, dealing with the unexpected). Assistance systems range from cobots and exoskeletons to AR glasses and AI advisors. Workers must be involved in the design so that systems support them rather than control them (see also 2.8.1).

Example: Exoskeletons from Ottobock, such as the Paexo for overhead work, have been tested in car plants to relieve the shoulders of workers who must hold their arms above their heads for long periods.

4.1.4 Semi-autonomous work environments

In semi-autonomous environments machines handle routine operation and people supervise, handle exceptions and improve the process. This is management by exception. It demands new skills (diagnosis, decision-making under time pressure) and new forms of organisation, such as teams responsible for a whole product flow. Risks are 'automation complacency' (people lose attention and skill) and the need to keep operators trained for manual intervention.

Example: In a highly automated plant such as Siemens' Amberg, where about three-quarters of the value chain is automated, employees mainly monitor processes, handle exceptions and carry out tasks that are not yet automated.

4.1.5 Flexible and family-friendly working hours

Digital tools can make working time more flexible: shift planning software, mobile access to schedules, and, for suitable jobs, remote work. In Germany this is shaped by collective agreements and law. The 2018 IG Metall agreement for the metal and electrical industry lets employees reduce their hours to 28 per week for up to two years, and the 2019 'Brückenteilzeit' law gives a right to return from part-time to full-time work. The challenge in production is that machines need staffing at fixed times, so flexibility has limits and must be negotiated.

Example: A plant uses an app on which team members swap shifts within defined rules, giving parents more control over their working times while keeping the line staffed.

4.1.A Dual vocational training system: adapting the apprenticeship model for digital skills

Germany's dual system combines training in a company with teaching at a vocational school. For Industrie 4.0 it is being modernised: training regulations are revised so that digital content (networking, programming, data) is standard, learning factories give apprentices hands-on experience, and companies add in-house digital modules. The system's strength is that content stays close to industry needs. The full description is in 4.2.1.

Example: Siemens' training sites teach apprentices on real automation components and simulation software, and offer extra courses in data and IT security.

4.1.B Lifelong learning programmes: continuous reskilling for existing workers

Because technologies change faster than people retire, skills must be renewed throughout a career. Programmes combine employer-organised courses, public funding (for example for further training under the Qualification Opportunities Act, 2019), learning on the job through AR assistance, and micro-credentials. A 'learning culture' means time, funding and recognition for learning. Details of the public framework are in 4.2.4.

Example: At Bosch's Blaichach plant, training of about 3,000 employees in new technologies accompanied the conversion to the networked factory.

The new profiles are not only for graduates; many can be reached through apprenticeship and further training. The table summarises typical tasks and skills.

4.2 Education and Skills Development

Germany's education system is a competitive advantage in Industrie 4.0. This section covers the dual system, the range of recognised occupations, continuing education and the push for digital and STEM skills.

4.2.1 Dual vocational training system

In the dual system young people (usually after lower or intermediate secondary school) sign a training contract with a company and attend a vocational school one or two days a week or in blocks. They receive a training allowance, learn practical work in the company according to a national training regulation, and sit examinations organised by the chambers of industry and commerce (IHK) or of crafts (HWK). Just under half a million new training contracts are signed each year. The model gives industry a skilled workforce and gives young people a route to employment, and it is regarded as a pillar of Germany's industrial strength.

Example: An apprentice industrial mechanic at a machine-building company spends three days a week in the workshop, one day at the vocational school and learns CNC programming and sensor technology as part of the updated curriculum.

4.2.2 300+ state-recognized apprenticeship programmes

Germany has around 320 state-recognised training occupations, maintained by the Federal Institute for Vocational Education and Training (BIBB) together with employers' associations and unions. They cover industrial occupations such as industrial mechanic, mechatronics technician, electronics technician for automation, IT specialist and industrial clerk. Regulations are revised from time to time to reflect new technology, for example the 2018 modernisation of metal and electrical occupations and the 2020 reform of IT occupations.

Example: The occupation 'Mechatroniker/in' was created in 1998 to combine mechanics, electronics and IT. It is now one of the most important occupations for the smart factory.

4.2.3 Continuous professional development

After initial training, skilled workers can progress through further-training qualifications: Meister (master craftsperson or industrial foreman), Techniker (state-certified technician), or, since the 2020 reform of the vocational training law, the titles 'Bachelor Professional' and 'Master Professional'. Public funding such as the 'Aufstiegs-BAföG' supports the costs. Companies and associations also offer courses for new technologies. The advantage is that experienced workers can move up without a university degree.

Example: A skilled worker becomes an industrial Meister with a course that now includes digital process control and data use, and then leads a production team.

4.2.4 Lifelong learning initiatives

Public initiatives support learning at all ages: the National Skills Strategy (2019) of the federal government, the social partners and the Länder; the 2019 Qualification Opportunities Act that funds training for employees whose jobs are changing; the 2023 law that introduced a 'Qualifizierungsgeld' for companies in structural change; paid educational leave in most federal states; and adult-education centres. The aim is to make learning a normal part of working life and to reach less-qualified groups.

Example: A company in structural change agrees with its works council on a training plan, and the Federal Employment Agency co-funds courses for the affected employees.

4.2.5 Digital skills training programmes

Digital skills programmes exist for school, vocational and adult level: the DigitalPakt Schule (2019) for equipment and teaching in schools, learning factories where students and trainees practise on real production lines, Mittelstand-Digital courses for SME staff, and the EU's DigComp framework for describing digital competences. In industry, companies run their own academies. The challenge is to reach people in small firms, which have little time and money for training.

Example: The Process Learning Factory CiP at TU Darmstadt is a real production line where students and company staff practise data-driven production optimisation.

4.2.A STEM education push: universities and technical schools for Industry 4.0

STEM, called MINT in German (mathematics, informatics, natural sciences, technology), is the base of Industrie 4.0 skills. Measures include early programmes such as 'Haus der kleinen Forscher', Girls' Day (since 2001) to attract girls to technical careers, dual-study programmes that combine a degree with company work, universities of applied sciences with a practical orientation, and new degree programmes in AI, data science and production informatics. Shortages of skilled people in technical occupations remain a major challenge.

Example: At Girls' Day, companies such as Siemens and Bosch open their plants for school girls to try programming and robotics, to widen the future pool of engineers.

4.3 Mittelstand 4.0 Support Programs

The Mittelstand is the backbone of the German economy, but small firms face special barriers in digitalisation. The lettered subpoints below explain three common barriers to adoption and the support available to smaller businesses.

4.3.A Challenges for small businesses: cost, complexity and digital literacy gaps

Small firms have limited money, few IT staff and little time. Typical barriers are unclear return on investment, complexity of choosing among many solutions and vendors, old machines without interfaces, concerns about IT security and data protection, and a shortage of staff with digital skills. Many also lack a digital strategy and start with tools rather than problems. Studies by VDMA and others show that SMEs adopt more slowly than large companies.

Example: A 40-person machining company knows that connecting its machines could help, but has no IT department, no clear idea which of dozens of platforms to choose, and fears that a failed project would cost more than it can afford.

4.3.B Government support programmes: grants, subsidies and consulting for SMEs

Federal programmes support SMEs with consulting, funding and loans. Examples: Mittelstand-Digital (free information, training and demonstration through regional centres), go-digital (consulting vouchers that subsidised external advice, from 2017), the Central Innovation Programme for SMEs (ZIM) for research and development projects, investment grants such as 'Digital Jetzt', and KfW development loans. Programme names, rates and eligibility change, so check the current conditions of the funding body before citing them.

Example: Through a consulting voucher, a toolmaker receives subsidised advice from a certified consultant to develop a plan for networking its machines and records.

4.3.C Industry 4.0 demonstration centres: showcasing best practices for smaller firms

Demonstration centres let SMEs see and try solutions before investing. They run real or model production lines, show practical cases, and provide neutral advice. The visitor sees the effect of, for instance, a simple retrofit sensor system, and talks to people at the same level, from companies like their own. Examples are SmartFactoryKL in Kaiserslautern, the Mittelstand-Digital centres, learning factories at universities, and the Fraunhofer institutes' open labs.

Example: A foundry owner visits a demonstration factory, sees how a small AI-based quality system works on a real line, and takes a plan home for a pilot at his own plant.

4.3.1 Regional centres of excellence

Regional competence centres bring advice close to the companies. The federal 'Mittelstand 4.0 Competence Centres' (from 2016) and their successor, the Mittelstand-Digital Centres, offer regionally anchored and thematically specialised services: workshops, demonstration, assessment and individual pilot projects. They are hosted by universities, research institutes and chambers, and they work free of charge for SMEs.

Example: A regional centre run by a university of applied sciences offers a half-day workshop in which local SMEs analyse one of their own processes and identify a first digital use case.

4.3.2 Digitalization of skilled crafts

The skilled crafts (Handwerk), such as carpenters, electricians, bakers and metalworkers, employ millions of people in small firms. Digitalisation here means digital customer contact and quotations, CAD/CAM and CNC tools, 3D scanning and measuring, building information modelling and mobile apps for site work. The Competence Centre for Digital Crafts supports the sector, together with the crafts chambers and the central association ZDH.

Example: A carpentry firm scans a customer's room with a 3D scanner, creates the design in CAD, and sends the data directly to its CNC machine for production.

4.3.3 SME awareness and training

Before investing, SMEs need to know where they stand and what is possible. Tools include readiness checks, for example the VDMA's 'Industrie 4.0 Readiness' study and acatech's Industrie 4.0 Maturity Index with six stages, from computerisation up to adaptability. Workshops, webinars, peer-to-peer learning and company visits raise awareness. Training for managers is as important as training for staff, since management decides on investment and priorities.

Example: Using a self-assessment checklist, a company discovers that it is at the 'connectivity' stage, and sets the next goal of making its machine status visible in real time.

4.3.4 Testbeds and demonstration factories

Testbeds are shared facilities where companies can test new technology with expert support and without disturbing real production. Demonstration factories show complete solutions. Examples are SmartFactoryKL, ARENA2036 in Stuttgart, the university learning factories and the Labs Network Industrie 4.0 (see 4.7.5). For SMEs, testbeds reduce the risk and cost of first experiments.

Example: A start-up and an SME test a new wireless sensor in the learning factory of a university for two weeks before deciding on a pilot at the SME's plant.

4.3.5 Mittelstand 4.0 agencies and multipliers

Multipliers are organisations that reach many SMEs: chambers of commerce and crafts, trade associations such as VDMA, ZVEI and Bitkom, regional development agencies and the thematic 'Mittelstand 4.0 Agencies' that the federal government funded from 2016 for topics such as cloud, processes, communication and trade. They translate research into practice, bundle questions and organise events and networks.

Example: A chamber of commerce organises a series of evening events with local SMEs, in which a Fraunhofer expert explains predictive maintenance and a local company shares its own results.

4.4 Government Initiatives and Funding

Public funding and regulation shape the pace of Industrie 4.0. The lettered subpoints below cover key funding, policy and regulatory themes.

4.4.1 'Autonomics for Industrie 4.0' programme

'AUTONOMIK für Industrie 4.0' was a technology programme of the federal economics ministry (then BMWi), which called for projects around 2013 and funded about a dozen collaborative projects over the following years. It followed the earlier AUTONOMIK programme. It supported autonomous, networked and simulation-based systems for industry, with a focus on SMEs, and was one of the first federally funded programmes for Industrie 4.0 technologies.

Example: Projects in such programmes developed, for example, assistance systems and self-organising production modules, and their results fed into standards and the Platform Industrie 4.0.

4.4.2 'Smart Service World' initiative

'Smart Service Welt' (Smart Service World) is based on the 2014 acatech recommendations and was funded by the federal economics ministry from 2015 as a technology programme. It aims at data-based digital services built on networked products: for example, remote maintenance, usage-based billing and platform services. The initiative reflects the shift from selling products to selling services (see 2.5).

Example: A project partnership in the programme develops a platform on which machine builders offer data-based services, such as monitoring and optimisation, to their customers.

4.4.3 €100 million in federal funding

An early federal funding figure of €100 million appears to refer to funding made available for Industrie 4.0 research and programmes in the initial phase. It should be quoted together with the programme and years to which it applies. Later schemes, such as Manufacturing-X with up to about €150 million, are of a similar scale. Public research funding is only a small part of total investment, because companies themselves spend far more.

4.4.4 Manufacturing-X funding scheme

Manufacturing-X is a cross-industry initiative to build open, decentralised data spaces for industry, where companies exchange data under their own control (data sovereignty). It grew out of the Platform Industrie 4.0 and builds on Catena-X in the automotive sector. The federal economics ministry published a funding concept in May 2023 and in August 2023 opened a funding call of up to €150 million. Projects include Factory-X (led by SAP and Siemens, for factory data) and Aerospace-X. Since 2025 the ministry has been renamed BMWE.

Example: Through Catena-X, a carmaker and its suppliers share quality and carbon-footprint data via standard connectors, and Manufacturing-X aims to extend this model to other sectors.

4.4.5 AI Action Plan and Industrial AI Strategy

The federal AI Strategy (2018, updated 2020) set aims for research, transfer, skills and regulation, with funding of several billion euros to 2025. It created national AI competence centres (for example in Berlin, Dortmund/Bonn, Munich, Tübingen and Dresden/Leipzig), and the Platform Lernende Systeme (2017) brings experts together. An AI action plan followed in 2023, and the 2025 Hightech Agenda continues the focus on AI. Industrial AI adds sector-specific themes: AI in production, trustworthy AI and data availability.

Example: A national AI competence centre works with a medium-sized machine builder to develop and test an AI model for quality inspection, giving the SME access to expertise and computing power.

4.4.A Government support programmes: grants, subsidies and consulting for SMEs

In addition to the SME programmes described in Section 4.3.B, general innovation funding is important: research grants from the federal research ministry, the ZIM programme, EU programmes (Horizon Europe, Digital Europe), the Länder’s own programmes and tax-based research allowances introduced in 2020. A good funding strategy matches the project stage (idea, prototype, pilot, scale-up) with the right instrument.

Example: A company develops an AI inspection prototype with a ZIM grant, tests it in a pilot with a regional programme and then finances the roll-out with a KfW loan.

4.4.B German government strategy: BMWK policies

The Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Climate Action (BMWK, since 2025 the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy, BMWE) is responsible for industrial policy, energy and the digital economy of industry, and runs the Platform Industrie 4.0 jointly with the research ministry. Policy lines include the National Industrial Strategy 2030 (2019), the funding of Manufacturing-X and Mittelstand-Digital, energy-cost relief for industry, and the support for the transformation to climate neutrality.

Example: The ministry funds both the Platform Industrie 4.0 secretariat and application projects, and links them through the Manufacturing-X initiative.

4.4.C EU regulations: GDPR, AI Act, Digital Services Act and others

European law has a strong effect on German industry. The GDPR (2018) governs personal data, including worker data. The AI Act (2024) regulates AI by risk level, with duties for high-risk systems that were postponed in the 2026 'Digital Omnibus' changes to December 2027 (stand-alone systems) and August 2028 (AI in regulated products). The Digital Services Act (2022) mainly regulates online platforms and marketplaces, so it affects industry mostly through B2B platforms. Other relevant laws are the Data Act (applicable from September 2025), the Cyber Resilience Act (2024; main duties from December 2027), the NIS2 Directive (2022; implemented in Germany in 2025) and the new Machinery Regulation (applicable from January 2027).

Standards make Industrie 4.0 interoperable. Example: A machine builder that follows the OPC UA standard for its data interface can connect its machine to control systems from different vendors without custom drivers.

Industrie 4.0 relies on partnerships between the state and companies. The Platform Industrie 4.0 combines government, industry, science and unions. The Fraunhofer Society is a publicly co-financed organisation that earns a large share of its income from industry contracts and runs testbeds and application centres. Both share the idea that neither the state nor the market alone can build the ecosystem. For further examples see 2.3.4 and 4.7.

Example: A Fraunhofer institute develops an inspection method with a car supplier. The state funds the basic infrastructure, and the supplier pays for the specific development.

4.5 Infrastructure Development

Digital factories need fast networks, reliable energy and shared research facilities. The subpoint below focuses on high-speed broadband as an essential part of factory infrastructure.

4.5.1 High-speed internet and 5G networks: industrial 5G, private 5G for factory floors

A private 5G network is a mobile network operated on the company's own site, often with its own licensed spectrum. In Germany the regulator (Bundesnetzagentur) assigned the 3.7–3.8 GHz band for local use, and later also the 26 GHz band, so factories and campuses can apply for their own frequencies. The advantages are controlled quality of service, low latency, independence from public networks and security under the company's own control. 5G complements rather than replaces wired networks and Wi-Fi, which remain standard for fixed machines.

Example: Bosch has tested 5G in its own plants, and Mercedes-Benz built an on-site 5G network into its Factory 56 in Sindelfingen to connect assembly, logistics and driverless vehicles.

4.5.2 Smart grid and energy infrastructure

A smart grid uses sensors, communication and software to balance electricity supply and demand, which is vital as Germany expands wind and solar power towards 80% of electricity consumption by 2030. Industry has a double role: it needs reliable, affordable power, and it can provide flexibility by shifting energy-intensive processes in time (demand response). Smart meters, energy-management systems and grid expansion such as the north-south HVDC lines are the infrastructure for this. High energy prices since 2022 have made industrial energy management a competitiveness issue.

Example: The aluminium producer Trimet operates its electrolysis lines so flexibly that they act like a 'virtual battery', absorbing surplus renewable power and reducing load when the grid is tight.

4.5.A High-speed broadband: backbone for rural and urban industrial zones

Many German manufacturers, including hidden champions, are located in rural areas, and they depend on fast, reliable internet connections for cloud services, remote maintenance and data exchange with customers. Germany has historically lagged behind many European countries in the share of fibre-to-the-premises connections. The federal Gigabit Strategy (2022) targets nationwide fibre by 2030, and funding programmes prioritise commercial and industrial areas. Until then, many SMEs rely on mobile networks or on-site edge computing to bridge the gaps.

Example: A small manufacturer in a rural area with a slow connection decides to process data on site with an edge device and only send summaries to the cloud.

4.5.3 Digital infrastructure investment

Digital infrastructure includes fibre, mobile networks, data centres, cloud capacity, data spaces and public digital services. Investment comes from telecom companies, cloud providers, the federal and state governments and the EU (Digital Decade targets, Digital Europe programme). In 2025 Germany agreed a special fund of €500 billion for infrastructure and climate neutrality over twelve years, with a part intended for digitalisation. Observers point to permitting delays and fragmented responsibilities as hindrances.

Example: A new data centre campus for a cloud provider, built near an industrial region, gives manufacturers there low-latency access to cloud services and AI computing.

4.5.4 Research facilities and test centres

Industrie 4.0 relies on shared research infrastructure: Fraunhofer institutes with application centres and testbeds, DLR facilities, university labs and learning factories, and high-performance computing. Germany's JUPITER at the Jülich Supercomputing Centre, opened in 2025, is Europe's first exascale supercomputer and offers capacity for AI and simulation. Test centres let companies validate new technology under realistic conditions before using it in production.

Example: A company uses a Fraunhofer testbed to try out private 5G with its robots before deciding on an installation in its own plant.

4.5.5 Innovation parks and technology hubs

Science and technology parks put start-ups, research institutes and large companies on one campus so that ideas spread quickly. German examples are Berlin Adlershof, the Garching campus near Munich, Cyber Valley in Tübingen/Stuttgart, Silicon Saxony in Dresden and the Innovation Park Artificial Intelligence (IPAI) in Heilbronn. Their value lies in proximity, shared facilities and the support of the surrounding universities.

Example: A start-up with an AI inspection system rents lab space in an innovation park, tests it at a neighbouring research institute, and wins a first customer from a company located on the same campus.

4.6 Social and Ethical Considerations

Industrie 4.0 changes working life. German policy and social partnership aim for a transformation that is human-centred, fair and participatory.

4.6.1 Charter for Work and Learning in Industry 4.0

This section summarises the work-and-learning principles promoted in Germany’s Industrie 4.0 discussions, including the Platform’s working group on work, education and training and the wider “Work 4.0” dialogue (including the Federal Labour Ministry’s 2017 White Paper). Common principles include: technology should serve people; employees should take part in designing change; qualification should be offered early and to everyone; working conditions should remain safe and healthy; and the gains should be shared fairly.

Example: A company that wants to introduce assistance systems first forms a working group with employee representatives, tests the systems in a pilot and trains staff before roll-out. This applies the charter's principle of participation.

4.6.2 Worker rights and data privacy

Connected factories can record what each worker does, which raises privacy issues. In Germany the works council has a right of co-determination on technical systems that can monitor behaviour or performance (Section 87(1) no. 6 of the Works Constitution Act), so such systems usually need a works agreement. Employee data are protected by the GDPR and the Federal Data Protection Act, which require a legal basis, purpose limitation and data minimisation. Good practice is to aggregate data, make rules transparent, and not use data for individual surveillance.

Example: A new MES records which operator started which job. The company and works council agree that data are used for traceability and process improvement, are not used for individual performance ratings, and are deleted after a set period.

4.6.3 Job security and transition support

Germany uses social partnership and public instruments to manage the transition. Short-time work (Kurzarbeit) bridges temporary demand drops; transfer companies help workers move to new jobs after restructuring; the Qualifizierungsgeld (2024) supports training in structural change; and collective agreements can exclude compulsory redundancies. Volkswagen and IG Metall agreed in December 2024 that there would be no compulsory redundancies until 2030 while staff numbers fall through voluntary measures.

Example: A supplier that is changing from combustion-engine parts to electric-drive parts retrains part of its staff with public co-funding and offers transfer to other plants for the rest.

4.6.4 Ethical AI implementation

Ethical AI means that systems respect human autonomy, fairness, transparency, safety and privacy. Reference points are the EU's Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (2019, seven requirements including human agency and oversight, technical robustness, transparency and non-discrimination), the German Data Ethics Commission (2019) and the legally binding AI Act (2024). Companies add their own codes: Bosch published an AI code of ethics in 2020, and SAP published guiding principles in 2018. In practice this means impact assessments, human oversight of critical decisions, and documentation.

Example: A company that uses AI to propose shift plans makes sure that the proposals can be explained and changed by the planner, and that the works council can see the rules that the system uses.

4.6.5 Inclusive digital transformation

Inclusion means that no group is left behind: older workers, women (who are under-represented in technical occupations), people with disabilities, workers with a migration background, and regions with weaker infrastructure. Digital tools can help, for example assistance systems that guide step by step, multilingual AR instructions or ergonomic aids, but they can also exclude if designed only for the young and tech-savvy. Inclusive design, accessible training and targeted programmes such as Girls' Day are part of the answer.

Example: A projection-based assembly assistant that shows each step directly on the workpiece lets people with learning disabilities in sheltered workshops carry out more complex assembly jobs.

4.7 Collaboration and Partnerships

Industrie 4.0 is a collective effort. This section describes the main networks that link companies, science, unions and other countries.

4.7.1 Plattform Industrie 4.0 network (300+ organizations)

The Platform brings together several hundred experts from companies, associations, research institutions, trade unions and government. Working groups cover reference architectures and standards, technology and application scenarios, security, legal frameworks, work and education, and digital business models. Results are published as papers and recommendations, and the Platform represents Germany in international cooperation. A steering structure with ministers and industry leaders sets priorities.

Example: The Platform's working group on legal questions published guidance on how existing contract and liability law applies to Industrie 4.0 scenarios, which helps companies and courts.

4.7.2 Industry-academia cooperation

Cooperation takes many forms: joint research projects, endowed professorships, shared labs and research campuses, dual-study programmes in which students alternate between university and company, doctoral work in industry and spin-offs. Germany's applied research organisation Fraunhofer is built around this link. The advantages are practical relevance for research and early access to talent for companies; the challenge is aligning time horizons and agreeing on IP rights.

Example: ARENA2036 in Stuttgart is a research campus where Daimler, Bosch, Festo, the University of Stuttgart and Fraunhofer institutes develop flexible vehicle production together, under one roof.

4.7.3 Trade union engagement

German unions, above all IG Metall, take an active part in shaping Industrie 4.0 through collective bargaining, works councils (co-determination) and seats in the Platform. Their themes are qualification, working time, data protection for employees, job security and the quality of work. The 2018 agreement on optional shorter working hours and the company-level agreements on technology are examples. Early union involvement tends to improve acceptance and to reveal practical problems.

Example: When a plant introduces data glasses for maintenance, the works council negotiates rules on how images and location data are recorded and used, and on training for affected employees.

4.7.4 International cooperation programmes

Germany cooperates internationally to promote its standards and to avoid fragmentation. Examples: the Hannover Declaration with Japan (2017); cooperation with China on Industrie 4.0 and 'Made in China 2025' agreed in 2015, although it is now more cautious; cooperation with the US Industrial Internet Consortium on reference architectures; the French Alliance Industrie du Futur; and EU initiatives such as the Digital Innovation Hubs and Digital Europe programme. The balance is between openness and protecting technology and security interests.

Example: Joint working papers of the Platform and the Industrial Internet Consortium compared RAMI 4.0 and the US reference architecture, to make it easier for companies to use both.

4.7.5 Labs Network Industrie 4.0

The Labs Network Industrie 4.0 (LNI 4.0), founded in the mid-2010s as an association, links test laboratories and research institutions that offer testbeds for Industrie 4.0 solutions, among them SmartFactoryKL and Fraunhofer institutes. Its aim is to let companies, especially SMEs, test products for interoperability and security with neutral partners, to organise joint tests (so-called plugfests) and to feed experience into standards.

Example: Two suppliers who want to show that their devices can work together test them in a network lab, find an interface problem, and correct it before the products go to customers.

4.8 Cybersecurity

Connected factories are exposed to cyber-attacks. This section brings together four cybersecurity themes, organised as sections 4.8.1 to 4.8.4.

4.8.1 Industrial Control System (ICS) security: protecting SCADA and PLC systems

ICS include SCADA systems, PLCs and distributed control systems that run physical processes. They were built for availability and safety, not security: older protocols such as Modbus lack authentication, devices run for decades without updates, and patching can need a production stop. Attacks can therefore stop production or damage equipment. Defences are network segmentation (the Purdue model with zones and a DMZ), asset inventory, hardened configurations, secure remote access and monitoring.

Example: Stuxnet (discovered 2010) targeted Siemens PLCs and damaged centrifuges, and a 2014 attack on a German steel mill, reported by the Federal Office for Information Security (BSI), prevented a blast furnace from shutting down properly and caused heavy damage.

4.8.2 Zero-trust architecture: securing interconnected factory networks

Zero trust assumes that no user, device or network segment is trusted by default; every access is checked ('never trust, always verify'), with least privilege and continuous monitoring (NIST SP 800-207). In factories this means identity for every device (certificates, as in OPC UA security), micro-segmentation per cell, strong authentication for remote access and logging. The challenges are legacy devices that cannot be authenticated, strict real-time and safety requirements, and the effort of managing identities. It is usually introduced step by step, starting with the most critical zones.

Example: A maintenance provider no longer connects to the whole plant network by VPN. Instead it receives a time-limited, logged access to one machine cell only, after multi-factor authentication.

4.8.3 Threat landscape: ransomware, espionage and sabotage risks

Manufacturing is one of the most attacked sectors. Ransomware encrypts systems and demands payment and often stops production; espionage steals designs and process know-how; sabotage and hacktivism aim to disrupt; supply-chain attacks reach many victims through one supplier. The BSI's annual situation reports describe the threat as high. Attackers often enter via office IT, phishing emails or remote-access tools and then move into production networks.

Example: In 2019 the aluminium producer Norsk Hydro was hit by ransomware and had to run plants manually for weeks, and in 2017 the NotPetya malware caused hundreds of millions of dollars of damage at companies such as Maersk.

4.8.4 Industrial cybersecurity (IEC 62443): protecting critical infrastructure and factory data

IEC 62443 is the main international standard series for the security of industrial automation and control systems. It has four groups: general (concepts, terms), policies and procedures (for asset owners and service providers), system requirements and component requirements, including a secure development lifecycle for manufacturers (62443-4-1). Central concepts are zones and conduits (grouping assets and controlling the connections between them) and security levels (SL 1 to 4). Certification bodies such as TÜV certify products and processes.

Some summaries describe it as a “zero-trust” standard. More precisely, the zones-and-conduits approach is compatible with zero-trust ideas, but IEC 62443 is a risk-based framework, while zero trust is an architectural principle that can be applied within it. The standard also supports compliance with NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act.

Example: A PLC manufacturer has its development process certified to IEC 62443-4-1 and its controller to 62443-4-2, so that plant operators can show that they used suitably secured components.

5.1 Digital Factory Architecture

How the smart factory is structured: the reference architecture, the digital description of every asset, the layers of IT and how everything is integrated.

5.1.1 Reference Architecture Model (RAMI 4.0)

RAMI 4.0 (Reference Architecture Model Industrie 4.0) is a three-dimensional model published in Germany as DIN SPEC 91345 in 2016. It helps to place any asset or function in a common frame. The three axes are: layers (asset, integration, communication, information, functional, business), life cycle and value stream (type and instance, from development to maintenance, based on IEC 62890) and hierarchy levels (product, field device, control device, station, work centre, enterprise, connected world, extending IEC 62264). The model is not an implementation; it is a map that shows where standards fit and where gaps remain.

Example: A temperature sensor can be located in RAMI 4.0: it is an asset on the field-device level, its data are described in the information layer, and its life cycle begins with the type 'sensor model X' and continues as an instance with a serial number.

5.1.2 Administration Shell concept

The Asset Administration Shell (AAS), German Verwaltungsschale, is the standardised digital representation of an asset: a machine, a component, a product or even a person or a process. It has an identity and contains submodels for different aspects, such as the digital nameplate, technical data, documentation, operating data or carbon footprint. It exists as a file (AASX) or as a live service with an API. The AAS is standardised as IEC 63278, and the Industrial Digital Twin Association (IDTA) publishes the submodel templates. It makes data exchange between vendors possible, and it can carry the Digital Product Passport.

Example: A manufacturer delivers an electric motor with an AAS file that contains its nameplate, datasheet, CAD file and carbon footprint, so the customer's engineering system can read everything automatically.

5.1.3 System integration layers

Traditional industrial IT is described by the automation pyramid (also known from the ISA-95 model): field level (sensors, actuators), control level (PLCs), supervisory level (SCADA, HMI), production management level (MES) and enterprise level (ERP). Industrie 4.0 does not abolish these functions, but it makes the strict hierarchy more flexible: devices can talk directly to the cloud or to other devices, and information flows across levels through standard interfaces such as OPC UA. Clear layer definitions still matter for security (zones) and responsibility.

Example: A sensor value moves from field level to a PLC, appears on the SCADA screen, is recorded by the MES against a production order and is finally used by the ERP system to calculate the cost of the batch.

5.1.4 Interoperability standards

Interoperability has levels: technical (connecting), syntactic (shared data formats), semantic (shared meaning) and organisational (agreed processes). Key standards are OPC UA (communication and information models), MQTT (lightweight messaging), the AAS (digital description), MTP (Module Type Package for process plants), VDA 5050 (driverless vehicles) and umati (a universal interface for machine tools, introduced by the VDW in 2019 as an OPC UA companion specification). Interoperability reduces integration effort and prevents vendor lock-in.

Example: With umati, a machine tool from one maker and another from a different maker both present their status, alarms and tool data in the same structure to a monitoring system, so only one connector is needed.

5.1.5 Vertical and horizontal integration

Vertical integration connects the levels inside a company, from shop floor to enterprise level (ERP, MES, SCADA), so that orders flow down and actual data flow up. Horizontal integration connects partners along the value chain, from suppliers via the factory to customers and service, for example in a shared data space. Together with end-to-end engineering (see 5.1.A) they form the three integration pillars of Industrie 4.0.

5.1.A End-to-end engineering: the digital thread from design to production to service

End-to-end engineering means that product and production data are created once and used throughout the life cycle, so that no data need to be re-typed. The digital thread links design (CAD, PLM), production planning, automation engineering, commissioning, operation and service. Data exchange formats such as AutomationML (IEC 62714) and the AAS help. Benefits are fewer errors, shorter development times and the ability to feed field experience back into design.

Example: Siemens' tools (NX for design, Teamcenter for product data, Tecnomatix for production planning, TIA Portal for automation) share data, so that a design change flows automatically into the production plan and the control program.

5.2 Core Technologies of Digital Factories

This section introduces the software and hardware building blocks of a digital factory. The lettered subpoints expand the unnumbered topics in this section.

5.2.1 Digital twins and virtual models

A digital factory uses three kinds of twin. The product digital twin is a virtual model of the product, from design to use. The production digital twin simulates machines, lines or the entire factory to plan and optimise operations. The performance digital twin monitors live operations and compares them with the model to find deviations and improve. Mature twins progress from a static model through a digital shadow with live data to a twin that can also control the real system.

Example: Siemens uses twins of both products and production in its plants, and BMW builds virtual factories in which planners test layouts and flows before construction. These are common use cases for virtual factory planning.

5.2.2 Product Lifecycle Management (PLM)

PLM software manages all product information from idea to end of life: requirements, CAD models, bills of materials, change orders, documents, variants and approvals. It ensures that everyone works with the current version and makes engineering changes traceable. Providers include Siemens (Teamcenter), PTC (Windchill), Dassault Systèmes (ENOVIA) and SAP. In Industrie 4.0 PLM is linked to production and service data, so that field data can improve the next product generation (closed-loop PLM).

Example: A carmaker approves an engineering change in PLM, and the change is automatically propagated to the bill of materials, the production plan and the supplier drawings.

5.2.3 Manufacturing Execution Systems (MES): bridging planning and production

An MES is the layer between planning (ERP) and the shop floor. Functions include detailed scheduling, dispatching orders to machines, collecting machine and operator data, tracking and genealogy (which materials went into which product), quality management, maintenance and performance analysis, as defined in ISA-95 and the German VDI 5600. German and international vendors include Siemens (Opcenter), SAP (Digital Manufacturing), Bosch (Nexeed) and MPDV (HYDRA). It makes production transparent in real time.

Example: If a supplier recalls a batch of screws, the MES shows within minutes which finished products contain screws from that batch, so only those need to be traced and checked.

5.2.4 Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP): connecting business processes with factory operations

ERP software integrates the business functions of a company: sales, purchasing, finance, inventory and production planning. SAP S/4HANA is the market leader in Germany. ERP integration with the shop floor means that orders and bills of materials flow to the MES and that confirmations, consumption and quality data flow back, so stock and cost are always up to date. Poor integration leads to manual data entry and delays.

Example: A customer order creates a production order in ERP. The MES schedules it, machines report quantities back, and ERP automatically books finished goods into stock and triggers the invoice.

5.2.5 Supply Chain Management (SCM) systems

SCM systems plan and control the flow of goods and information from suppliers to customers. They cover planning (demand planning, sales and operations planning, supply planning), execution (warehouse and transport management) and collaboration with partners. Examples are SAP Integrated Business Planning, Blue Yonder and o9. Good SCM reduces the 'bullwhip effect', where small changes in demand cause large swings in orders upstream.

Example: A planning system combines forecasts, stock levels and supplier lead times and warns that a critical component will run short in six weeks, so the buyer can order from an alternative supplier in time.

5.2.A SCADA and PLC modernization: upgrading legacy systems for Industry 4.0

Most existing plants run SCADA systems and PLCs that are 15 to 30 years old. Modernisation options are: replace (new controllers and software), retrofit (add sensors, gateways and edge devices to publish data), or wrap (put an OPC UA server in front of old systems). New developments include IEC 61499-style distributed control, virtual PLCs running as software on industrial PCs, and web-based SCADA. The decision depends on risk, cost and downtime, and security updates are a major driver.

Example: A plant adds an edge gateway that reads data from an old PLC and offers it via OPC UA, so a new MES can use the data without changing the control program.

5.2.B RFID and sensor networks: tracking materials and products throughout production

RFID tags and barcodes or data-matrix codes identify parts, containers and products automatically. Low-frequency, high-frequency and UHF RFID differ in range and cost; NFC is used for short-range reading. Real-time locating systems with ultra-wideband or Bluetooth locate tools and vehicles on the shop floor. Together with sensor networks they give a live picture of where things are and what has been done to them.

Example: In Siemens' Amberg plant, each circuit board carries a code, so the product itself tells the machines which processing steps it needs. BMW tracks vehicle bodies through its plants with RFID.

5.2.C Human-robot collaboration: cobots on the factory floor and safety systems

For safe collaboration, the robot cell is protected by layers of safety technology: safety laser scanners and light curtains that detect people and reduce speed or stop, safety-rated controllers with protocols such as PROFIsafe, force and torque limitation inside the robot, and wearable or camera-based proximity detection. A risk assessment of the whole application, following ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066, decides which measures are needed. See 3.3.5 for the collaboration modes.

Example: A cobot that places parts in a fixture slows down when a worker enters the zone within one metre, and stops if the worker comes closer, as monitored by a laser scanner.

5.3 Smart Production Systems

How a digital factory actually produces: self-controlled processes, adaptable lines, real-time optimisation and quality. Letters A and B refer to the un-numbered points at the end of this section.

5.3.1 Self-controlled production and self-optimizing processes

In self-controlled production, decisions are taken locally by machines and products instead of by a central planner: a workpiece asks for the next free machine, and machines negotiate priorities. Combined with self-optimisation (see 1.3.1), the system tunes itself to goals such as throughput or energy use. Multi-agent systems and the AAS provide the technical basis. The benefit is robustness and flexibility; the challenge is to guarantee predictable global results.

Example: At SmartFactoryKL, products with their own memory select among available production modules, so the line adjusts automatically to a new order sequence.

5.3.2 Adaptive manufacturing lines: machines that reconfigure based on orders

Adaptive lines reconfigure automatically for each order or product variant: tools and fixtures are changed, robot programs are loaded, modules are moved and material flows are redirected. Techniques are quick-change systems, reconfigurable modules on driverless vehicles, and software that downloads the right program from the order data. This supports small batches and mass customisation (see 2.2).

Example: KUKA's matrix production concept and the modular assembly trials of car makers replace the fixed assembly line by free-standing cells that are served by driverless vehicles and can be rearranged.

5.3.3 Real-time process optimization

Real-time optimisation adjusts process parameters while production runs, using sensor data and models. Techniques are adaptive control, model predictive control (MPC), which predicts the process behaviour over a short horizon and chooses the best inputs, and ML-based setpoint recommendations. It is established in process industries, such as chemicals and steel, and spreads to discrete manufacturing. It requires reliable data, a validated model and safe limits.

Example: In a rolling mill, a model predicts the thickness of the strip and adjusts roll gap and tension every few milliseconds, which reduces thickness variation and scrap.

5.3.4 Quality Management 4.0

Quality Management 4.0 moves from inspecting finished products to preventing defects. Elements are inline measurement and vision inspection, statistical process control with real-time data, full traceability (genealogy), closed-loop correction of processes and predictive quality models. It also integrates the quality system (ISO 9001) with MES data. The quality function changes from controlling to analysing and improving.

Example: Zeiss supplies inline measuring systems that check car-body dimensions during production, and deviations immediately trigger a correction on the upstream process rather than waiting for the final audit.

5.3.5 Zero-defect manufacturing

Zero-defect manufacturing (ZDM) aims to avoid defects completely by combining four strategies: detect (find defects early), repair (rework them), predict (forecast quality) and prevent (control the causes). Zero defects is a direction rather than a measurable state, but world-class plants approach it: Siemens reports a quality rate of 99.99885% in Amberg, about 11.5 defects per million, close to Six Sigma level (3.4 per million). EU projects such as the Zero Defect Manufacturing Platform (ZDMP) developed supporting tools.

Example: At Amberg, almost every product is tested automatically, test results are fed back into the process, and data analysis identifies where defects could arise, so that the cause can be removed.

5.3.A Intelligent warehousing: AGVs, smart shelves and robotic picking

Intelligent warehousing combines automated guided vehicles for transport, shuttle and shelf systems for storage, smart shelves with weight or RFID sensors for stock tracking and robotic picking arms with vision. Warehouse management software controls everything and optimises slotting and routes. Linking the warehouse to the MES and ERP ensures that material arrives at the line on time. See also 1.4.3.

Example: A warehouse robot with a camera picks small parts from bins and puts them into kits for the assembly line, supported by AI trained on thousands of part images.

5.3.B Real-time production scheduling: dynamic adjustments based on demand and capacity

Real-time scheduling re-plans production whenever something changes: a rush order arrives, a machine breaks down, material is late. Advanced planning and scheduling (APS) software uses current data from the MES and optimisation algorithms to compute a new feasible plan within seconds or minutes. The aim is high delivery reliability and use of capacity without constant manual re-planning.

Example: When a machine stops unexpectedly, the scheduling system automatically moves its jobs to a similar machine, re-orders the queue, and warns the planner about an order that will be late.

5.4 Connected Logistics and Material Flow

Inside the factory, material must arrive at the right place at the right time. This section covers the technology of internal logistics.

5.4.1 Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs)

AGVs are driverless vehicles that move loads along defined routes. Types include tugger trains, unit-load carriers, forklift AGVs and under-ride vehicles. Guidance technologies are magnetic tape, inductive wires, laser reflectors and natural navigation by contour. A control system assigns missions and manages traffic and charging. The guidelines of VDI 2510 describe such systems in German practice. AGVs work well on stable routes with repetitive transport.

Example: In a car plant, a train of AGVs delivers pre-assembled cockpit modules to the line every few minutes, in the exact sequence of the cars.

5.4.2 Driverless transport systems

Driverless transport systems (FTS) is the German umbrella term for AGVs and AMRs together with their control system. A modern FTS connects to ERP, MES and warehouse management, so that transport orders are generated automatically by production demand. The VDA 5050 standard allows vehicles from different manufacturers to be coordinated by one master control. Important design topics are safety (people and vehicles in shared areas), traffic planning and the integration with doors, lifts and conveyors.

Example: At Mercedes-Benz's Factory 56, driverless transport vehicles carry vehicle bodies and parts between stations, and the route can be changed by software, without altering the floor.

5.4.3 Smart warehouse management

A smart warehouse management system (WMS) controls goods receipt, put-away, storage, picking and dispatch, using real-time stock and order data. Functions include dynamic slotting (storing fast-moving items near the exits), optimised pick routes, wave planning and integration with automation. A digital twin of the warehouse allows testing changes. SAP Extended Warehouse Management is widely used in German industry.

Example: A WMS notices that two products are often ordered together and moves them to neighbouring locations, which cuts walking and picking time.

5.4.4 Just-in-Time (JIT) and Just-in-Sequence (JIS) delivery

Just-in-time delivers parts exactly when needed, which minimises inventory. Just-in-sequence goes further: parts arrive in the exact order in which they will be assembled, for example seats in the order of the cars on the line. Both rely on reliable data exchange, short transport times and close supplier cooperation. The weakness is fragility: without buffers, any disruption quickly halts the line.

Example: In 2016 a dispute between Volkswagen and two suppliers stopped deliveries, and production at the Wolfsburg and Emden plants was halted for about a week, which shows the vulnerability of JIT chains.

5.4.5 RFID and tracking technologies

RFID, barcodes, GPS and real-time locating systems track goods inside and outside the plant. In logistics, standards such as GS1 (barcodes and EPC for RFID) allow companies to identify items uniquely, and returnable containers, such as the VDA small load carriers (KLT) used in the car industry, are tagged to track their circulation. Tracking data feed inventory, transport and billing systems.

Example: Tagged load carriers circulating between a supplier and a car plant are automatically scanned at each gate, so both sides see stock, location and the number of empties to be returned.

5.5 Flagship Digital Factory Examples (Real-World Case Studies)

Five well-known plants illustrate both the achievements and limits of digital production. These examples include BMW Leipzig and the Volkswagen Transparent Factory, each discussed in terms of its development and current role.

5.5.1 Siemens Electronics Works Amberg (EWA)

The Electronics Works Amberg, opened in 1989 in Bavaria, produces SIMATIC controllers and other automation products and is widely described as one of the most advanced digital factories. Key figures, as reported by Siemens: a quality rate of 99.99885% (about 11.5 defects per million), around 75% automated value creation, around 17 million products a year (about one per second) in roughly a thousand variants, and tens of millions of data points collected every day. The plant is also a showcase for the digital twin: products and production are planned virtually, and the products carry codes that tell the machines what to do.

Because the plant makes automation equipment, it also serves as a test bed and showroom for Siemens' own products. Humans remain essential for the roughly 25% of work that is not automated and for exceptions.

Key points

  • 99.99885% quality rate (about 11.5 defects per million)
  • Around 75% automated production
  • Digital twin of product and production
  • Industry 4.0 showcase facility

Example: A circuit board enters the line with a unique code. At each station the machine reads the code, retrieves the matching instructions from the system, performs its step and records the result, so each of the thousand-plus product variants is handled correctly without manual set-up.

5.5.2 Bosch Connected Factory: the Blaichach plant

Bosch’s Blaichach plant is a prominent example of Industrie 4.0 adoption and award recognition. The plant at Blaichach-Immenstadt in the Allgäu makes ABS and ESP brake-control components. It started its Industrie 4.0 conversion in 2013 and received a national “Landmark in the Land of Ideas” award in 2015. Thousands of machines are networked (about 6,600 connected devices in 2021), data are evaluated with Bosch’s own software, and a global production network for ABS/ESP shares experience. According to Bosch, output tripled in six years without expanding the plant, and about 3,000 employees were trained.

Key points

  • IoT-enabled production: thousands of machines on a common network
  • Cross-plant networking within the ABS/ESP production network
  • Predictive maintenance systems
  • Employee training in parallel with technology

Example: Predictive maintenance warns that a machine is drifting, a maintenance technician is automatically informed on a mobile device, and the machine is serviced during a planned stop instead of failing during a shift.

5.5.3 Adidas Speedfactory

Adidas opened its Speedfactory in Ansbach, Germany, in 2016 and a second one in Atlanta, USA, in 2017. They used robots, 3D printing and automated knitting to make sports shoes close to the customer, with the goal of shortening the time from idea to shop shelf from months to days and enabling rapid customisation and local production. But Adidas closed both Speedfactories in 2019–2020, moved the technology to its Asian suppliers and stated that the concept was not flexible or economic enough at scale.

The case is valuable because it shows the limits: automation does not remove cost differences by itself, the products have to match the technology, and local production needs strong demand to be viable.

Key points

  • Automated footwear production
  • Localised manufacturing
  • Rapid customisation capability
  • Closed in 2019–2020: a lesson about scalability and economics

Example: The technology developed in the Speedfactories, for example 3D-printed midsoles, lives on in Adidas' product range, even though the factories themselves have closed.

5.5.A BMW's Leipzig plant: flexible production for electric and combustion vehicles

BMW's Leipzig plant opened in 2005. Its central building, designed by Zaha Hadid, lets car bodies travel through the offices, and the plant is known for flexibility: models with combustion engines, plug-in hybrids and fully electric drives are produced on the same line. Leipzig built the carbon-fibre BMW i3 and i8, which required new processes, and it uses wind turbines on site for part of its electricity. Flexibility reduces the risk of uncertain electric-vehicle demand because one line can adapt the mix.

Example: On the same line, a customer's electric car follows a customer's petrol car, and the system supplies the right battery or engine and the right parts to each station at the right time.

5.5.B Volkswagen's Transparent Factory (Dresden)

The glass-walled Transparent Factory opened in Dresden in 2002 to build the Phaeton, and was designed as a showcase where customers could watch the final assembly and collect their car. Parts were brought into the city centre by a freight tram, the CarGoTram. It later built the e-Golf (2017–2020) and the ID.3. The idea of customer-centric, digitally integrated production was attractive, but volumes were small (about 6,000 cars a year, against more than 500,000 in Wolfsburg), and vehicle production ended in December 2025. The site is to become a research centre for chips, AI and robotics, with the Technical University of Dresden using about half of it.

Key points

  • Customer-centric, transparent production in a city centre
  • Digitally integrated logistics with the CarGoTram
  • Production ended in 2025; converted to a research hub

Example: Customers could watch their car being assembled behind glass and collect it at the plant, which created a strong brand experience that was costly to run at low volume.

5.6 Implementation Roadmap

Most companies cannot rebuild a factory; they have to change step by step. A typical roadmap has five stages.

5.6.1 Assessment and readiness analysis

The first step is to find out where the company stands and what it needs. A readiness analysis looks at processes, machines, IT and OT systems, data, skills and culture, and compares them with a maturity model such as acatech's Industrie 4.0 Maturity Index (computerisation, connectivity, visibility, transparency, predictive capacity, adaptability) or the VDMA readiness check. Baseline figures such as OEE, scrap and downtime are measured so that later improvement can be proved. The result is a prioritised list of use cases linked to business goals.

Example: A company finds that it is at the 'connectivity' stage: most machines are networked, but data are not yet analysed. It decides that the next goal is machine-status visibility for the three bottleneck lines.

5.6.2 Pilot projects and proof of concept

A pilot tests a solution on a small scale with clear success criteria, a limited budget and a time frame of a few months. It should address a real pain point and have a committed sponsor and users. Pilots reduce risk and build internal support. A frequent trap is 'pilot purgatory', in which many pilots are started but none is scaled up, because the business case or the plan for roll-out is missing.

Example: A manufacturer fits vibration sensors to one critical press, tests predictive maintenance for four months and measures the reduction of unplanned stops before deciding to extend it.

5.6.3 Scaling and integration

Scaling takes a proven solution to more lines and plants. It requires standardised architecture and data models (for example with the AAS), a platform for deployment and updates, a replication method and local ownership. Integration with ERP, MES and security systems must be solved properly rather than by ad-hoc interfaces. Bosch's dual strategy shows the path: it proved the solutions in lead plants such as Homburg and Blaichach and then rolled them out across more than 250 plants.

Example: After the successful pilot on one press, the company defines a standard sensor kit and data format and installs it on all presses of the same type in three plants.

5.6.4 Change management

Technology projects fail more often because of people than because of technology. Change management addresses communication, participation, training and leadership: explaining the purpose, involving employees and the works council early, training users before go-live, and celebrating early successes. Models such as Kotter's eight steps or ADKAR provide structure. In Germany, co-determination makes early involvement of the works council a legal and practical must.

Example: Before rolling out a tablet-based shop-floor system, a company runs workshops with operators, adapts the screens to their suggestions and names 'digital champions' on each shift to help colleagues.

5.6.5 Continuous improvement

Digitalisation is not a one-time project. Continuous improvement combines lean thinking (Kaizen, the PDCA cycle) with data: dashboards reveal losses, teams analyse root causes, test changes and measure results. 'Digital lean' uses real-time data to make improvement faster and fairer. The system itself must also be updated, for example with new sensors, models and software versions.

Example: A team reviews the weekly OEE dashboard, sees that changeovers are the biggest loss on line 3, redesigns the changeover process and checks the following week whether the loss has fallen.

5.7 Standards and Security

A digital factory needs common communication standards and security from the start. This section covers the main requirements.

5.7.1 Unified communication standards

Connected factories need shared languages. OPC UA (IEC 62541) is the leading standard for secure, platform-independent communication with built-in information models and 'companion specifications' for industries and machine types. MQTT (ISO/IEC 20922) is a lightweight publish-subscribe protocol, popular for sensor data to the cloud. Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) adds deterministic behaviour to Ethernet. Fieldbus standards such as PROFINET and EtherCAT remain for real-time control. The trend is OPC UA over TSN as a unified solution.

Example: A machine publishes its data as an OPC UA server with a standard model, and an MES, an analytics tool and a mobile app each read what they need without special drivers.

5.7.2 IT security architectures

Security architecture uses defence in depth: several layers, so that one failure does not give access to everything. Typical elements are network segmentation into zones with firewalls (the Purdue model), a demilitarised zone between IT and OT, strong identity and access management, endpoint protection, monitoring, and an information security management system following ISO/IEC 27001. The architecture must consider production needs such as availability and real-time behaviour.

Example: The plant network is divided into cells. Traffic between the office network and the production network passes through a monitored DMZ, and only defined connections are allowed.

5.7.3 Data protection and GDPR compliance

In factories the GDPR applies when data relate to identifiable people: operator IDs, access logs, camera images, wearables or location tracking. Requirements are a legal basis, purpose limitation, data minimisation, transparency, security and, for risky processing, a data protection impact assessment. Works councils have co-determination rights (see 4.6.2). Pure machine data are generally not personal, but they can become so if linked to an operator.

Example: A company that uses cameras to monitor a safety zone configures the system to blur faces and delete recordings after a short period, and documents this in a works agreement.

5.7.4 Cybersecurity for networked systems

Protecting networked production needs technical and organisational measures: maintaining an inventory of assets, patching or compensating controls for unpatchable devices, secure remote access for vendors, offline backups, monitoring with OT-aware tools, and an incident-response plan. Regulation increases the duties: NIS2 requires risk management and incident reporting for many manufacturers, and the Cyber Resilience Act sets security requirements for products. Training of staff is needed against phishing.

Example: A maintenance service provider connects to a machine only through a managed access gateway with multi-factor authentication, which logs the session and closes it after the job.

5.7.5 Security-by-design principles

Security-by-design means building security into products and systems from the start instead of adding it later. The Platform Industrie 4.0 and the IEC 62443-4-1 standard call for secure development processes: threat modelling, least privilege, secure defaults, secure boot, signed firmware updates, a hardware root of trust and a software bill of materials (SBOM). The EU Cyber Resilience Act makes many such practices legally binding for manufacturers of connected products.

Example: A controller manufacturer signs its firmware cryptographically, so that the device accepts only authentic updates and refuses modified software.

5.8 Testing and Validation

New technology should be tested before it is used in production. Germany has a dense landscape of facilities for this.

5.8.1 Testbeds at universities and research centres

Testbeds are experimental production environments at universities and research centres where new technologies can be tried with expert support. Examples are the learning factory CiP at TU Darmstadt, the labs of RWTH Aachen's machine tool laboratory (WZL) and its Campus Cluster Smart Logistics, and Fraunhofer institutes such as IPA in Stuttgart. They allow SMEs access to equipment they could not afford and provide students with practical training.

Example: A company tests a new robot-vision system on a testbed at a Fraunhofer institute and receives a report on accuracy and reliability before deciding to invest.

5.8.2 Demonstration factories

Demonstration factories are complete production environments built to show what is possible and to test solutions in a realistic setting. SmartFactoryKL in Kaiserslautern, supported by many industrial and research members, and the ARENA2036 research campus in Stuttgart are well-known. Visitors can see technology working, ask questions and compare options. Demonstration factories also serve as a place where vendors test interoperability.

Example: At SmartFactoryKL, visitors saw the 'Production Level 4' line where modules from different suppliers were combined and re-ordered automatically.

5.8.3 Living labs

A living lab (German Reallabor) tests innovation in real conditions with real users, often in a regulatory sandbox that temporarily relaxes rules to allow experiments. The federal government's strategy for 'Reallabore' (2019) supports this. In industry, living labs may be real production lines on which a research institute and a company test new solutions together, with continuous feedback from workers.

Example: A port authority tests a digital platform for truck slot booking on the real traffic of a terminal for six months, then evaluates it with the truckers and terminal operators.

5.8.4 Simulation environments

Simulation environments allow testing without risk or cost of real hardware. They include discrete-event simulation of material flows, physics-based robot simulation (for instance in NVIDIA Isaac Sim), virtual commissioning (control software tested against a virtual machine) and hardware-in-the-loop tests where a real controller is connected to a simulated plant. They shorten start-up times, find errors early and allow training of operators and AI models in safe virtual settings.

Example: Before a new line is installed, the PLC program is tested against a virtual model of the line, which reveals logic errors, so the real commissioning takes days instead of weeks.

5.8.5 Real-world pilot applications

Real-world pilots take a validated solution into live operation under controlled conditions. Good pilots define success criteria and measurement beforehand, include the people who will use the solution, keep a fallback to the old process, and collect lessons for scaling. They should run long enough to cover typical variation, such as different materials and shifts.

Example: Bosch's Homburg plant served as a lead plant where solutions for connected manufacturing were tested in real production, and the lessons were then transferred to its other plants.

5.9 Performance Metrics and KPIs

What cannot be measured cannot be managed. These are the key measures of a smart factory, with simple worked calculations. Letters A and B refer to the two un-numbered points at the end of this section.

5.9.1 Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE): measuring smart factory performance

OEE is the most widely used measure of how well equipment is used. It multiplies three factors: Availability (run time divided by planned production time), Performance (actual output divided by the output possible at ideal speed) and Quality (good parts divided by total parts). An OEE of about 85% is often called world class (a benchmark usually credited to Seiichi Nakajima), but typical plants are lower. OEE shows where the losses are: stops, slow running or defects.

5.9.2 Production efficiency gains

Efficiency measures include throughput (units per hour), cycle time, labour productivity (output per hour worked), first-pass yield, scrap and rework rates, and energy or material use per unit. Industrie 4.0 gains come from less downtime, faster changeovers, fewer defects and better use of materials and energy. Company claims such as Bosch's tripled output at Blaichach should be read with care: they describe a specific plant over a specific period.

Example: If a line makes 1,000 units per day with 40 hours of labour, labour productivity is 25 units per hour. If automation and better scheduling raise output to 1,200 with the same hours, productivity rises by 20%, to 30 units per hour.

5.9.3 Cost reduction metrics

Cost metrics include cost per unit, cost of poor quality (scrap, rework, warranty), maintenance cost as a share of asset value, inventory carrying cost, energy cost per unit and logistics cost. Digital systems make it possible to allocate costs to individual orders and machines. A cost reduction should be tested against the cost of the technology itself, otherwise savings may be overstated.

Example: If scrap falls from 3% to 1.5% on a product with a material cost of €20 per unit and 500,000 units a year, the saving is about 0.015 x 500,000 x €20 = €150,000 a year.

5.9.4 Time-to-market improvements

Time-to-market is the time from the idea or order to the product being available to the customer. Digital design, simulation and virtual commissioning, additive prototyping, integrated engineering data and flexible production all shorten it. Measures are development lead time, time from order to delivery (order lead time) and time to ramp up a new product to full volume.

Example: A company that uses virtual commissioning cuts the start-up of a new line by three weeks and so brings a new model to market earlier than competitors.

5.9.5 Return on Investment (ROI)

ROI measures the return of an investment: (gain minus cost) divided by cost. For projects with several years, companies also use payback time and net present value. For Industrie 4.0 investments some benefits are hard to quantify (flexibility, resilience, data for future use), so business cases should include conservative estimates and track actual results after the project.

Example: A sensor system costs €200,000 and saves €80,000 a year. Payback is 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.5 years. Over three years the savings are €240,000, so ROI = (240,000 - 200,000) / 200,000 = 20%.

5.9.A Quality and defect rates

Quality indicators include defects per million opportunities (DPMO), parts per million (PPM) defective, first-pass yield, scrap and rework rates and process capability (Cpk). A Six Sigma process has 3.4 DPMO. Smart factories use inline measurement to capture these values continuously and trace each defect to its cause.

Example: Siemens' Amberg plant reports a quality rate of 99.99885%, which equals 11.5 defects per million, so only about one in 87,000 products is defective.

5.9.B Time-to-market and flexibility indices

Flexibility can be expressed with indices such as changeover time (SMED aims to reduce it below ten minutes), the number of variants a line can produce without conversion, the minimum economic batch size and the speed of reacting to demand changes. These indices matter for mass customisation. Together with time-to-market they show how quickly a factory can adapt, which is one of the main goals of Industrie 4.0.

Example: Amberg produces about a thousand product variants with frequent changeovers on the same lines. A plant that can change variant in minutes, not hours, has a high flexibility index.

6.1 Data Management and Analytics

Data are the raw material of Industrie 4.0. These five topics apply to every chapter above.

6.1.1 Data governance

Data governance sets the rules for how data are owned, defined, accessed, protected and used. It assigns roles (data owners and stewards), defines policies and standards, maintains a data catalogue and tracks data lineage (where data come from and how they are changed). In industrial settings it also covers who may use machine data from customers' plants, which the EU Data Act now regulates. Without governance, data projects end in disagreements over definitions and access.

Example: A manufacturer defines 'scrap rate' in one way for all plants, names an owner for the quality data and records in a catalogue which systems produce and consume them.

6.1.2 Data quality management

Data quality has dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness. Industrial data have special problems: sensor drift, missing values, wrong time stamps, different units and manual entry errors. Methods include validation rules at the source, automatic checks, calibration, cleansing and monitoring of quality indicators. The saying 'garbage in, garbage out' applies strongly to AI, which learns from whatever data it is given.

Example: A predictive-maintenance model gives false alarms because two sensors on a pump use different time zones, so their data are misaligned. A simple check of time stamps solves it.

6.1.3 Real-time analytics

Real-time analytics evaluate data as they arrive rather than after storage. Stream-processing engines apply rules or models to the incoming data and trigger alerts or actions within seconds. It is used for live OEE, quality alerts, predictive-maintenance warnings and dynamic scheduling. Architecture often combines the edge (fast, local) with the cloud (deep, historic) in a so-called lambda or kappa pattern.

Example: A stream-processing job compares each new temperature reading of an oven with a rolling average, and sends an alert to a maintenance technician as soon as the deviation passes a limit.

6.1.4 Data visualization

Visualisation turns data into pictures that people can understand quickly: trend lines, control charts, Pareto charts of loss causes, heat maps, Sankey diagrams of energy or material flows, and 3D views of a digital twin. Good visualisation shows the right information to the right person: a clear KPI for the manager, a detailed trend for the engineer. Poor visualisation (too many colours, no context) hides rather than reveals.

Example: A Sankey diagram of a plant's energy use shows at a glance that compressed air and heating take the largest share, and so directs the efficiency programme to them.

6.1.5 Knowledge management

Knowledge management captures and shares what people and systems know. In industry this is urgent because many experienced workers retire and take tacit knowledge with them. Tools include maintenance wikis, lessons-learned databases, knowledge graphs (which link machines, parts, faults and fixes) and, increasingly, AI assistants that search company documents and answer questions. A culture of sharing is as important as the tools.

Example: A knowledge graph connects a fault code to its typical causes and repairs from past service reports. A technician asks an assistant and gets a ranked list of probable causes.

6.2 Standardization and Interoperability

Without standards, every connection is a custom project. Standards make parts from different makers work together.

6.2.1 International standards (ISO, IEC)

Global standards are created by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), often in joint committees. Relevant fields are automation (IEC TC 65), industrial data and systems (ISO TC 184), IoT (ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 41), safety (IEC 61508, ISO 13849) and security (IEC 62443). Key documents include IEC 62264 (the enterprise-control integration series based on ISA-95), IEC 61131 (PLC programming) and IEC 61499 (distributed control). Germany contributes through DIN and DKE.

Example: IEC 61131-3 defines programming languages for PLCs, so an engineer trained on one vendor's system can understand programs written for another.

6.2.2 Industry-specific standards

Many sectors add their own rules on top of the generic ones: automotive (VDA 5050 for driverless vehicles, Catena-X data standards, ISO 26262 for functional safety), process industries (NAMUR recommendations, the Module Type Package), machine tools (umati), packaging (PackML), electronics assembly (IPC-CFX and IPC-HERMES-9852 for machine communication) and pharma (GMP rules). Industry-specific standards often move faster than formal international ones because the users shape them directly.

Example: With IPC-HERMES-9852, circuit-board assembly machines from different manufacturers pass boards to each other and exchange the information about the board without custom interfaces.

6.2.3 Communication protocols (OPC UA, MQTT)

OPC UA is a platform-independent standard for secure industrial communication with a rich information model: it describes not only values but also what they mean and how devices are structured. MQTT is a very light publish-subscribe protocol: devices publish messages to topics on a broker and subscribers receive them, which suits many small devices and unreliable connections. They are complementary: OPC UA is strong for modelling and machine-to-machine integration, MQTT for efficient data transport to cloud and IT. Both can be combined, and OPC UA also offers a publish-subscribe mode.

6.2.4 Semantic interoperability

Semantic interoperability means that both sides understand the meaning of data, not just the format. It uses shared vocabularies and ontologies, such as ECLASS, the German-developed standard for classifying products and describing their properties (also an ISO/IEC standard family), and the IEC Common Data Dictionary. In the Asset Administration Shell, each property carries a semantic identifier that points to such a dictionary. This allows machines and software to interpret data automatically.

Example: Two systems both use the ECLASS identifier of a property 'maximum operating temperature', so one system reads the value from the other's data sheet in the right unit and meaning.

6.2.5 Standardization Council Industrie 4.0

The Standardization Council Industrie 4.0 (SCI 4.0), founded in 2016 by Bitkom, DIN, DKE, VDE, VDMA and ZVEI, coordinates the standardisation work on Industrie 4.0. It identifies needs, brings stakeholders together, maintains the German Standardization Roadmap and helps to carry German proposals into international committees. It also cooperates with similar bodies abroad.

Example: SCI 4.0 coordinated the German input that helped OPC UA companion specifications and the Asset Administration Shell to become part of international standards work.

6.3 Cybersecurity and Resilience

Security is a cross-cutting duty (see also 4.8 and 5.7). This section looks at the main security processes and at AI-based security.

6.3.1 Threat detection and prevention

Prevention reduces the chance of attacks (patching, hardening, segmentation, training), and detection finds those that get through. Tools are network monitoring and intrusion detection tuned for OT protocols, security information and event management (SIEM), endpoint detection, threat intelligence and regular vulnerability scans. The first requirement is knowing what is on the network: an up-to-date asset inventory.

Example: A monitoring system learns the normal communication pattern between a PLC and its engineering station and raises an alert when an unknown computer suddenly sends commands to the PLC.

6.3.2 Incident response

Incident response is the organised handling of a security event: prepare, detect and analyse, contain, eradicate, recover and learn (following frameworks such as NIST SP 800-61). In production the first priority is safety, then availability. Plans must define roles, communication (including legal reporting duties such as under NIS2) and ways to run manually. Regular exercises reveal weak points.

Example: When Norsk Hydro was attacked in 2019, it switched plants to manual operation and informed the public openly and regularly, which is often quoted as a model of crisis communication.

6.3.3 Business continuity planning

Business continuity planning ensures that critical operations can continue or be restored quickly after a disruption, whether a cyber-attack, fire, power failure or supplier failure. It starts with a business impact analysis and sets recovery time and recovery point objectives, and uses redundancy, offline backups, alternative sites and suppliers, and tested restoration. ISO 22301 is the standard for such systems.

Example: After the 2017 NotPetya attack, Maersk had to reinstall thousands of servers and tens of thousands of PCs. It managed within about ten days, helped by one domain controller in Ghana that had been offline during the attack.

6.3.4 Secure-by-design systems

Secure-by-design systems apply security principles from the first design step: least privilege, defence in depth, fail-safe defaults, minimal attack surface, separation of duties and secure updates. Methods such as threat modelling (for example STRIDE) identify risks early. For makers of connected products, the Cyber Resilience Act requires many of these practices and obliges them to handle vulnerabilities over the product's life. See 5.7.5 for the product side.

Example: During the design of a new gateway, engineers list possible attacks on each interface, decide that only encrypted connections will be accepted, and close all unused ports by default.

6.3.5 AI-based security (KIbSIS project)

AI is used for security in two ways: AI for security (finding anomalies and attacks) and security of AI (protecting AI systems). KIbSIS (KI-basierte Sicherheit für Industrie-Systeme, AI-Based Safety and Security for Industrial Systems) is a research project within the EU's IPCEI-CIS programme. Its partners are Fraunhofer AISEC, Siemens (coordinator), TRUMPF and Wibu-Systems. It develops AI-based anomaly detection and automated security functions for edge devices, security for AI in industrial settings and quantum-resistant protection, with the aim of contributing to Germany's and Europe's digital sovereignty. One use case is automated AI-based safety acknowledgement for machine tools.

Example: An AI model at the edge learns the normal behaviour of a machine tool's network traffic and control commands, and automatically isolates the machine if it detects an abnormal command sequence.

6.4 Legal and Regulatory Framework

Law shapes what companies can do with data, AI and automated systems. This section gives an overview; it is not legal advice.

6.4.1 Intellectual property rights

Industrial innovation is protected by patents (for inventions), copyright (software), design rights and trade secrets, which in Germany are protected by the Trade Secrets Act (2019, implementing an EU directive). Data are not owned like a thing; they are protected indirectly through contracts, trade secret law and, since September 2025, the EU Data Act, which gives users of connected products rights of access. For digital twins and 3D printing, rights in design files and models need clear contracts.

Example: A machine builder and a customer agree in the contract who may use the data generated by the machine, for what purpose and for how long, to avoid disputes about improvements based on that data.

6.4.2 Liability in automated systems

When an automated system causes harm, responsibility may lie with the manufacturer, the system integrator, the operator or the software provider. The new EU Product Liability Directive (2024) explicitly covers software and AI and must be transposed into national law by the end of 2026, and it extends liability to defects arising after delivery through updates. A proposed EU AI Liability Directive was dropped in 2025. Documentation, logging and risk assessments are central to proving diligence.

Example: If a collaborative robot injures a worker, an investigation checks whether the robot design, its integration into the cell, the safety settings or the operator's instructions were at fault.

6.4.3 Contract law for digital services

Digital services raise questions of service levels, data use, updates, liability and termination. German civil law has special rules for contracts on digital products (Sections 327 ff. of the Civil Code, since 2022, implementing an EU directive). Industrial contracts for platforms, software-as-a-service and outcome-based models should specify performance and availability (SLA), data ownership and portability, security duties, IP in improvements, and exit conditions.

Example: In a compressed-air-as-a-service contract, the supplier guarantees a minimum air quality and availability, and the customer pays by volume. The contract also defines who owns the consumption data.

6.4.4 Compliance requirements

A manufacturer of connected, AI-enabled products faces several sets of rules at once: product safety and CE marking (Machinery Regulation), cybersecurity (NIS2, Cyber Resilience Act), data (GDPR, Data Act), AI (AI Act), supply chains (Supply Chain Due Diligence Act) and environment (ecodesign, digital product passport). A compliance management system with a register of obligations, responsible persons, controls and audits is the practical answer. Standards such as ISO 9001, ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO 50001 help to demonstrate compliance.

Example: A company keeps a matrix of its products against regulations, so for each new connected product it knows which assessments, documents and labels are required before it can be sold.

6.4.5 Cross-border data flows

Industrial companies transfer data between countries in global plants, cloud services and supply chains. The GDPR allows transfers of personal data outside the EU only with a legal basis, such as an adequacy decision (for instance the EU-US Data Privacy Framework of 2023) or standard contractual clauses. Other countries, such as China, have their own data export and localisation rules. Data spaces and sovereign cloud offerings, such as Gaia-X, are meant to give companies more control.

Example: A German company with a plant in China must check what production and employee data may be sent to its headquarters, and under which conditions, according to Chinese and EU rules.

6.5 Economic Impact and Business Value

In the end, Industrie 4.0 has to pay. This section explains how to evaluate the economics and what strategic effects follow.

6.5.1 Cost-benefit analysis

A cost-benefit analysis compares all costs (hardware, software, integration, training, running costs) with all benefits (less downtime, scrap and energy; higher output; avoided investment), over the life of the project. Common indicators are payback time, net present value (NPV) and ROI. Include risks and sensitivity: what if savings are half as large? Intangible benefits (flexibility, data for the future) should be listed separately.

Example: A predictive-maintenance project costs €160,000 (sensors 60k, software 40k, integration 50k, training 10k) and saves €120,000 a year (100k from 40 fewer downtime hours at €2,500 per hour, 20k in maintenance). Payback is 160 / 120 = 1.33 years. With an 8% discount rate, the NPV over three years is about €149,000.

6.5.2 Competitive advantage

Industrie 4.0 can create advantage through lower cost (efficiency), higher quality, faster delivery, greater flexibility and new services. Porter's strategies of cost leadership and differentiation both benefit. But technology alone is easy to copy; lasting advantage comes from combining it with unique know-how, data and customer relationships, as in the 'lead supplier' concept.

Example: A machine builder whose machines deliver data that help customers improve their processes becomes a partner rather than a supplier of equipment, which makes it harder for low-cost competitors to replace.

6.5.3 Market disruption

Digital technology can disrupt established markets. New entrants use software and platforms to take over customer relationships (as described in Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation), and incumbents risk becoming suppliers of interchangeable hardware. Examples are Tesla's challenge to the car industry, with large castings and software updates, and the rise of Chinese electric-vehicle makers. German firms respond with partnerships, platforms and by moving into software and services.

Example: Tesla's Berlin-Brandenburg factory uses very large aluminium castings that replace dozens of parts, which simplifies assembly and pushes established makers to rethink body construction.

6.5.4 Value chain optimization

Using Porter's value chain, Industrie 4.0 improves each activity (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing, service) and the links between them through shared data. A digital thread from design to service, supplier integration and real-time visibility reduce waste and delay. Optimisation across companies, as in Catena-X, is harder than inside one firm, because partners must trust each other with data.

Example: A carmaker and its suppliers share demand forecasts and stock levels in real time, so suppliers produce what will be needed, and the carmaker needs less safety stock.

6.5.5 New business opportunities

Industrie 4.0 opens opportunities for established firms (digital services, retrofits, platforms) and for start-ups (analytics, AI inspection, marketplaces, carbon accounting, robotics). It also creates a market for consulting, training and integration. Mittelstand firms can turn their know-how into digital products. The challenge is to build the new business with a separate culture and a different time horizon from the core business.

Example: A machine tool maker founds a software unit that sells production-planning apps to other manufacturers, including some that do not own its machines.

7.1 Current State of Germany 4.0

Fifteen years after the term was coined, what has been achieved and what has not?

7.1.1 Adoption rates (17% of German companies in 2023)

Some estimates put adoption at 17% of German companies in 2023. Adoption figures depend on the survey and on what is counted: Industrie 4.0 applications in general, specific technologies such as IoT or AI, or only manufacturing firms. Bitkom, ZEW and the statistics office (Destatis/Eurostat) publish different numbers, so the source should be named. A consistent finding is that large companies adopt faster than small ones, and that many firms use isolated solutions rather than fully integrated systems.

Example: Large carmakers and suppliers run networked plants with MES and analytics. A typical 50-person machine shop may have a few networked machines and uses a spreadsheet for planning, and so would count as an adopter in some surveys but not in others.

7.1.2 Success stories and lessons learned

Success stories include Siemens Amberg (standardised data and digital twins), Bosch Blaichach (people-first transformation), Kaeser (a new business model with compressed air as a service) and many SME retrofits. Failures also teach: Adidas' Speedfactory and the limits of Volkswagen's Transparent Factory show that technology must fit the business case.

7.1.3 Challenges and barriers

The main barriers are: shortage of skilled workers; limited resources and know-how in SMEs; legacy machines and IT/OT systems; missing interoperability; cybersecurity risks; a heavy regulatory and bureaucratic burden; high energy prices since 2022; slow broadband and 5G roll-out; mistrust about data sharing; and a weak investment climate, with the German economy stagnating or contracting in 2023 and 2024. Many of these are not technical but organisational, financial and political.

Example: A mid-sized firm that wants to retrofit sensors finds that it cannot hire an IoT specialist, that the energy price squeezes its margins, and that the investment must compete with other urgent projects.

7.1.4 Global positioning

Germany remains strong as a lead supplier of automation, machines and standards, and has world-class showcase plants. It is weaker in software platforms, cloud and AI at scale (dominated by US firms), and in the speed and scale of Chinese manufacturing and electric-vehicle development. In robot density Germany is among the leaders, but China overtook it in 2023. In innovation rankings Germany is usually in the top ten. The position is therefore strong but contested.

Example: German machine builders supply factories worldwide, while the large cloud platforms that these factories increasingly rely on are mostly American, which is why data sovereignty initiatives matter.

7.2 Future Trends

Five trends will shape the next stage.

7.2.1 Industry 5.0: human-centric manufacturing

The European Commission's 2021 policy paper on Industry 5.0 complements Industry 4.0 with three pillars: human-centricity (technology serves workers' needs and wellbeing), sustainability (within planetary boundaries) and resilience (robust to shocks). It does not mean a fifth technological revolution; rather, it adds goals and values to the technology of Industry 4.0. Examples are cobots and exoskeletons that reduce strain, AR for skill support, circular production and secure supply chains.

Example: A plant that introduces AI scheduling asks not only 'does it reduce cost?' but also 'does it give staff more predictable shifts and does it reduce energy use?' That is an Industry 5.0 question.

7.2.2 Quantum computing applications

Expected uses are optimisation in logistics and production planning, simulation of new materials and batteries, and, with quantum sensing, very precise measurements. Quantum computers also threaten today's public-key cryptography, so industry must prepare for post-quantum cryptography (NIST published the first standards in 2024). KIbSIS's focus on quantum-resistant protection (see 6.3.5) shows that German industrial research already addresses this. Practical advantage for production problems is still to be shown.

Example: A manufacturer begins to list which of its devices use long-lived cryptographic keys, to plan their replacement with quantum-resistant methods over the next years.

7.2.3 Advanced AI and machine learning

Trends include foundation models adapted to industry (for engineering, maintenance and planning), generative design, AI agents that carry out multi-step tasks, physics-informed ML that combines data and physical laws, synthetic data for training, and edge AI. Their reliability, safety validation, transparency and compliance with the AI Act decide how fast they enter safety-critical areas. Human oversight stays essential.

Example: An AI agent receives a delivery delay notice, checks stock and the production plan, proposes three re-planning options with their costs, and waits for a planner to choose.

7.2.4 Sustainable and circular manufacturing

Regulation and markets push in the same direction: carbon pricing (EU ETS), the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (definitive phase from 2026), the Digital Product Passport, supply-chain due diligence and customer demand. Industrie 4.0 supplies the data and tools: energy and material monitoring, product passports on the Asset Administration Shell, remanufacturing logistics, and green hydrogen or electrification of heat. Sustainability becomes a core design criterion for factories.

Example: A manufacturer of electric motors adds a digital product passport that records materials and repair history, so that at end of life the motor can be remanufactured or its magnets recycled.

7.2.5 Global collaboration and standardization

The challenge is to keep interoperability while geopolitical tensions, export controls and 'tech blocs' push toward fragmentation. Germany and the EU promote open standards and data spaces (Manufacturing-X, Catena-X) and bilateral cooperation. The success of the AAS as an IEC standard and OPC UA companion specifications show that global standards are still possible. Balancing openness with security and technological sovereignty is the central policy issue.

Example: A global supplier delivers a component with an AAS that all customers' systems can read, regardless of which country or platform they use.

7.3 Recommendations

Five groups can each contribute to the success of Germany 4.0. The recommendations below follow from the analysis in this document.

7.3.1 For policymakers

Priorities are to complete fibre and 5G coverage, especially in industrial and rural areas; to secure affordable and reliable energy; to reduce bureaucracy and give legal certainty (stable timelines for the AI Act, clear data rules); to continue funding testbeds, Mittelstand-Digital and Manufacturing-X; to support skills and lifelong learning; and to use public procurement and standards to create markets for trustworthy, interoperable technology.

Example: A simple one-stop service for SMEs that combines funding applications for digitalisation and energy efficiency would reduce the paperwork that deters smaller firms.

7.3.2 For industry leaders

Leaders should link digital projects to business strategy; build the data foundation and adopt open standards (OPC UA, AAS) to avoid lock-in; protect operations through security by design; involve employees and works councils early; develop service and data-based business models; track results with clear KPIs such as OEE and ROI; and prepare for regulation and sustainability requirements.

Example: A CEO appoints a cross-functional team of production, IT, finance and works-council representatives to own the roadmap, with quarterly reviews against agreed KPIs.

7.3.3 For SMEs

SMEs should start small with a clear problem, use the free advice and demonstration offers of Mittelstand-Digital and the Fraunhofer institutes, retrofit existing machines rather than replacing them, prefer standard interfaces and open systems, secure the basics (updates, backups, access control), join networks and associations to learn from peers, and invest in training of a few 'digital champions'.

Example: A 60-person manufacturer starts with machine-status monitoring on its three bottleneck machines, learns from the results and expands step by step.

7.3.4 For educational institutions

Schools, vocational schools and universities should integrate data and digital skills in all technical programmes; run learning factories and projects with industry; offer interdisciplinary courses (mechanics, electronics, IT, ethics); provide short, modular further-training options; keep curricula up to date with industry together with the chambers and BIBB; and promote STEM careers among under-represented groups.

Example: A university of applied sciences and a local company co-develop a part-time certificate in industrial data analysis for employees, which is taught partly in the company's own production hall.

7.3.5 For workers and unions

Workers should take up training opportunities and take part in the design of new systems; works councils and unions should negotiate agreements on data use, qualification and working time, and keep up with the technology so that they can judge it; and both should insist that productivity gains are shared fairly. Strong social partnership is a competitive advantage for Germany, not an obstacle.

Example: A works council negotiates a company agreement that guarantees training time for every employee affected by a new automation system and limits the use of performance data.
